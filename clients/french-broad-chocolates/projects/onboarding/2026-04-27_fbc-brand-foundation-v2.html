Section 01 · Brand Foundation

Two voices.
One chocolate brand.

French Broad Chocolates does not sound like a chocolate company. It sounds like two people who have spent twenty years arguing affectionately about cacao — one telling stories with their hands covered in chocolate, the other patiently dismantling a marketing simplification. That difference is the brand.

  • The animating beliefChocolate is a food. Pleasure is the point. If a draft contradicts either, it's wrong.
  • Specificity over abstractionAlways names, places, numbers. Cacao Bisiesto, Matagalpa. 39 inches up the door. 12 hours of conching.
  • People, place, and processThe founders' three-part lens. Used spontaneously, often. Use it when explaining anything about cacao.
  • Story-rooted claimsA claim never floats. Sustainability isn't a value statement; it's a 39-inch waterline and a production floor 3 ft higher.
  • Anti-pretension"I will never turn up my nose at a milk chocolate bar, or a creamy peanut butter cup, or what I will go to my grave believing is the best dessert in the world — a chocolate chip cookie."
  • Asheville as a characterNot a backdrop. The terroir of Asheville is influencing the business. Named locals — Counter Culture, Sandy Mush, Mountain BizWorks.
  • We do all of it, and we do it deeplyThe most-repeated competitive line. A four-beat rhythm: source / make / transform / invite.
  • The published three-pillar promiseSourced with integrity. Crafted with intention. Served with gratitude. The cleanest unifying triplet — already in production.

Universal Rules

Banned phrases.
Must-include patterns.

Each rule is sourced to a founder quote. These aren't preferences — they're rules the founders have personally enforced.

Banned

Phrases we don't use.

  • FBCIn any external copy. Internal shorthand only. Jael, on record.
  • Decadent · sinful · indulgentAs primary descriptors. Treats chocolate as transgression.
  • Guilt-free"There's nothing to feel guilty about."
  • Luxury as bare descriptor / headline frameBanned in "luxury chocolate," "luxury experience." Permitted when subordinated to a moment, e.g., Jael: "a beautiful, small luxury in a moment that transports you."
  • Premium · artisanalAs standalone claims. Show the work instead.
  • Direct trade without nuanceDan: "impossibly reductionist." Use carefully and qualify.
  • Synergy · leverage · disruptOff-register. Not a food brand.
  • Generalizations like our farmersWithout naming. Name them.
Must include · at least one per piece

Patterns to keep.

  • A named person, place, supplier"Cacao Bisiesto in Matagalpa." "Counter Culture, two blocks over." "Step Family Farms raspberries."
  • A scene or momentNot an abstract claim. Truffles up to elbows. 39 inches at the door.
  • A sensory verbRooted in eating, not marketing. Melts. Blooms. Lingers. Snaps. Releases. Opens.
  • A community gestureWhen the piece is consumer-facing.
  • A qualificationWhen the piece is sourcing or sustainability. Keeps it from sounding like greenwash.
Structural patterns
  • The four-beat brand stanzaSource · make · transform · invite. Spine of the brand promise.
  • Hard fact → soft reframeJael move. Lead with the hard reality, end on the human reframe.
  • Tidy frame → "actually it's more complex" → quiet definitiveDan move. Lead conventional, dismantle, restate.
  • Three-pillar mappingBrand-level copy maps to Sourced · Crafted · Served.

" Resilience · canonical · Jael

The Helene story

"Hurricane Helene's flood happened on September 27, 2024… She rose, and rose, and rose, and breached her banks, and crossed the street, and climbed the hill, and climbed the steps to our front door… The water reached thirty-nine inches up our front door. Production sat 3 ft higher and stayed dry — the only reason we're still here. We sold every last piece of inventory we had. People bought gift cards. They bought subscriptions. Pastry chefs were working in packaging. Baristas in shipping. Our whole company learned to live a day in someone else's shoes."

"What is a Bonbon?" — pillar page lead

A bonbon is a small chocolate sculpture with a soft filling and a snappy chocolate shell. That's the technical answer. The longer answer is that a bonbon is a playground.

Our Head Chocolatier Edgar calls the production line a choreography. Twenty thousand bonbons leave our Asheville kitchen every week, made by a seven-person team using empty-mold rehearsals, hand-tempered cocoa butter, paintbrushes flicked backwards into a starburst.

Inside the shell — handmade in batches of around five thousand, monitored for sugar concentration and water activity. Three months without a single preservative.

Section 03 · Audience

Six segments.
Three lines of business.

French Broad Chocolates runs three distinct lines that touch different buyers. Each segment has its own purchase context, lead message, and voice routing. Where a segment is partially inferred rather than directly named in founder transcripts, it's flagged.

Segment 01

DTC chocolate buyer

Confirmed · subscription & e-commerce

The repeat e-commerce buyer who orders for themselves. Often started as a tourist who visited the Lounge or Factory; now lives elsewhere and re-orders chocolate as a small ritual.

What they actually buy
A reliable five-minute ceremony. Permission to slow down. A reason to think about Asheville without leaving home.

Proof

Cross-segment patterns

Four through-lines
across all six audiences.

i

Story-led purchase

Across all six segments, customers are buying with a story, not despite it. Even Segment 4 (the most utilitarian) chooses FBC over commodity bulk because of who and what is behind the bag.

Implication: every audience-specific page should foreground a name, a place, or a moment in the first 50 words.

ii

Asheville is an asset

For three segments (DTC, Gift, Walk-in) Asheville is part of the value. For two (Retail B2B, Foodservice) it's a credible-source signal. For one (B2B Cacao) it's incidental.

Implication: lean into the geography for the first three. Don't fight it for the latter two.

iii

Relationship-led economics

Subscription rather than one-off. Long-term wholesale rather than transactional. Co-import rather than spot-buy. The brand is built for high-LTV / low-CAC dynamics.

Implication: marketing should match — invest in audience depth over reach.

iv

Pleasure as the through-line

The single emotional through-line across every segment is pleasure-as-vital, not pleasure-as-transgression. From the Tuesday-night ritual to the wholesale buyer choosing "people-pleasing" over "connoisseur-only."

Implication: if a piece of audience-specific copy could read as joyless, it's wrong, regardless of segment.

Architectural pattern

Hub-vs-pillar split

Two clusters separate the navigation hub from the content pillar that drives traffic. Documented pattern, not exception.

Hub (e.g., /learn/pairings/) — thin nav page. Site architecture + internal-linking equity. Doesn't target a high-volume keyword.

Pillar (e.g., /learn/pairings/chocolate-and-wine-pairings/) — the content asset that captures organic traffic. Targets the head term.

Where it applies

Two clusters use this pattern

Asheville Local SEO + Pairings. Reserved for clusters where the natural hub keyword is thin but the cluster has clear high-volume spoke intent.

Asheville — Hub: /asheville/ · Pillar: /asheville/chocolate-guide/ targeting "asheville chocolate" (2,900/mo).

Pairings — Hub: /learn/pairings/ · Pillar: /learn/pairings/chocolate-and-wine-pairings/ targeting "chocolate and wine" (8,100/mo).
