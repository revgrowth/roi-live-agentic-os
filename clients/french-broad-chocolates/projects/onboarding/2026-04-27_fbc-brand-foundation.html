Brand Foundation

The voice reflected back.

French Broad Chocolates does not sound like a chocolate company. It sounds like two people who have spent twenty years arguing affectionately about cacao — one of them telling stories with their hands covered in chocolate, the other patiently dismantling a marketing simplification. That difference matters. This is how the voice works.

Why it sounds the way it sounds

Two animating beliefs do all the load-bearing. Everything else descends from them.

First — chocolate is a food. Not a luxury, not a guilty pleasure, not a sweet. A food shaped by people, place, and process, just like wine, coffee, or bread. Second — pleasure is the point. Source seriously, craft seriously, then make something deeply delicious. Never make pleasure the prerequisite for understanding.

If a draft tilts toward purity-as-superiority — toward the connoisseur lecture — it is off-brand. If a draft tilts toward decadence and indulgence and luxury — toward the Lindt commercial — it is also off-brand. The voice lives in the narrow lane between them, and it stays there because the founders have personally policed it for two decades.

Chocolate is a food just like grain is a food. One of the principal purposes of food is pleasure. — Jael, on The Southern Fork

The shared brand voice — eight load-bearing patterns

These show up across both founders and the published work. They are the patterns that make a paragraph read as French Broad Chocolates regardless of who voiced it.

  1. The animating belief. Chocolate is a food and pleasure is the point. If a piece of writing implicitly contradicts either, it's wrong. Source — Jael, repeatedly across origin and competitive transcripts.
  2. Specificity over abstraction. Always names, places, numbers, dates, processes. "Cooperativa Norandino." "39 inches up the front door." "$7–$10 per kilogram." "12 hours of conching." Source — Jael and Dan, across all transcripts. Both founders refuse the generic.
  3. People, place, and process — as a structural device. The founders use this triplet spontaneously and often. Use it when explaining anything about cacao, chocolate, or the company's choices. Source — Jael on The Southern Fork: "It is not only a plant, it is people, and it is place."
  4. Story-rooted claims. A claim never floats. Sustainability isn't a value statement; it's a 39-inch waterline and a production floor sitting three feet higher. Connection-to-source isn't a slogan; it's a school bus driven from Minnesota to Costa Rica with a four-month-pregnant founder. Source — both founders, in the lived detail of every long answer.
  5. Anti-pretension. The voice actively dismantles expertise-as-gatekeeping. "I will never turn up my nose at a milk chocolate bar, or a creamy peanut butter cup, or what I will go to my grave believing is the best dessert in the world — a chocolate chip cookie." Source — Jael, competitive transcript.
  6. Asheville as a character, not a backdrop. The river is the central metaphor; the practical anchor is the named local community — Counter Culture coffee, Sandy Mush strawberries, Mountain BizWorks, Grove Park Inn. Source — Jael, origin transcript: "We believe the terroir of Asheville is influencing our business."
  7. We do all of it, and we do it deeply. The most-repeated competitive line. The four-beat rhythm — flat declarative, refrain, list of acts — is a spine to use, not a tagline to retire. Source — Jael, competitive transcript. Verbatim.
  8. The published three-pillar promise. The website distills the founders' sprawl into the cleanest unifying triplet — sourced with integrity, crafted with intention, served with gratitude. New brand-level copy should map to one or transparently bridge two of the three. Source — frenchbroadchocolates.com About page.
Voice signature · Jael

Story-driven, sensory, refrain-built.

Jael is the brand's primary public-facing voice. She does the founder narrative, the customer-emotional register, the slow-down-and-feel-it tone. She talks to Mother's Day shoppers, podcast listeners, journalists writing a profile, and a wholesale buyer who needs to feel a story.

Five identifying patterns
  • Refrain-builds. "We do all of it, and we do it deeply." "Joy, connection, offering." "People, place, and process." Phrases that swing into the rhythm of the sentence.
  • Three-beat phrasing. "Beautiful and messy; frustrating and deeply fulfilling; unpredictable and humbling."
  • Reframing — from absence to presence. She redirects rather than lectures. "I want food to bring me closer to who I am."
  • Universe-conspires register. She frames consequential decisions as recognized signs. "Chocolate found me." Real, not affected.
  • Self-disclosure that loops back to product. A personal story, then the brand returns at the close — without a hard sell. The Mother's Day blog is the canonical demonstration.

Canonical example · anti-reductionism

"Direct trade is a very convenient marketing term, but it is probably impossibly reductionist — because cacao necessarily passes through many hands… Direct trade with French Broad Chocolates means we try to know the nuance of those stories of origin. Voice signature · Dan

Process-deep, anti-reductionist, technical with humanism.

Dan is the brand's process-deep voice. He does sourcing, manufacturing, ethics, and the unglamorous truth of supply chains. He talks to wholesale buyers asking real questions, industry peers, journalists writing about cacao economics, and consumers who want the unsanitized version.

Five identifying patterns
  • Anti-reductionism as a stance. Openly suspicious of marketing simplification, including the brand's own. "I've been growing allergic to reductionist messages."
  • Real-time self-correction. Says it, hears it, adjusts. "Let me strike that. It's not to have the greatest control possible. It's to have the greatest reliable partnership possible." In writing: lead with the conventional framing, then explicitly correct it.
  • Specific names + specific quantities, dense. "$7–$10 per kilogram." "12 and a half metric ton container." "12 hours of conching." "0.5% lecithin by weight."
  • Tender humanism inside technical narrative. The softest moments are inside the most technical answers. "I don't know that I have ever visited a cacao farm that wasn't proud of the cacao they produced."
  • Big-picture leveling. "We are not separate from commodity. We are all connected." The antidote to craft-chocolate exceptionalism — used when the brand voice is in danger of sounding self-congratulatory.

Universal rules

Banned phrases, must-includes, structural patterns. Each is sourced to a specific founder quote — these aren't preferences, they're rules the founders have personally enforced.

Banned

  • FBC in any external copy — Jael, explicit: "I don't want it to show up in any marketing copy ever."
  • decadent · sinful · indulgent as primary descriptors — chocolate is a food, not transgression.
  • guilt-free — "There's nothing to feel guilty about."
  • luxury as a bare descriptor or headline frame ("luxury chocolate," "luxury experience"). Permitted when founders/team use it in qualified phrases that subordinate the word to a specific moment, product, or sensory frame. Banned use: "Experience the luxury of French Broad Chocolates." Permitted use (Jael): "a beautiful, small luxury in a moment that transports you."
  • premium as a standalone claim — show, don't tell.
  • artisanal as a vague descriptor — name the specific craft instead.
  • farm to table — overused. Use connection to source or bean to bar.
  • direct trade without nuance — Dan: "impossibly reductionist." Use carefully and qualify it.
  • synergy / leverage / disrupt and corporate jargon — off-register entirely.
  • Generalizations like "our farmers" without naming — name them.

Must include — at least one per piece

  • A named person, place, or supplier. "Cacao Bisiesto in Matagalpa." "Daniel South in Puerto Viejo." "Counter Culture coffee, two blocks over."
  • A scene or moment — not an abstract claim.
  • A sensory verb or feeling-word rooted in eating, not in marketing. Melts, blooms, lingers, snaps, releases, opens.
  • A community or belonging gesture when the piece is consumer-facing.
  • A qualification or honest limit when the piece is sourcing/sustainability — to keep it from sounding like greenwash.

Structural patterns

  • The four-beat brand stanza. Source · make · transform · invite. Spine of the brand promise.
  • Hard fact → soft reframe — Jael move. Lead with the hard reality, end on the human reframe.
  • Tidy frame → "actually it's more complex" → quiet definitive — Dan move. Lead conventional, dismantle, restate.
  • Three-pillar mapping — when brand-level, map explicitly to Sourced with integrity / Crafted with intention / Served with gratitude.

How a bonbon is made, start to finish

Confections · canonical · two voices
Edgar: "We start off with clean molds. Imperative for allergen control. And then we take those molds, we garnish them — hand tempering the cocoa butter, and using various techniques. Hand painting. Spraying with a spray gun. And using different unique tools that we kind of made up. I've taken paintbrushes and smashed them back the opposite way they're supposed to be, and that creates, like, a flower motif or what we call starburst."

Rihanna: "We garnish them, and then we flood them using kind of like a conveyor belt technique where one person is in charge of filling each mold with chocolate and then handing them off to the next person, then dump that out to get the shell thickness that we're looking for. Then the third person scrapes the molds and monitors the machines, making sure that they're in a good temper, and the holes are full and making sure that there's no blooming happening with the shells."

Edgar: "What makes it harder than people think — the very simple motions that you have to make in everything that we do. A new person sees us doing it and they're like, looks easy. And then they go to do it and chocolate will kind of spill everywhere, and then they have a little bit of a panic. So what we've had to do is establish almost a choreography for new people."

Rihanna: "We're all kind of doing quality control at every step of the way. Each person has to meet that standard of excellence at their steps so that they're passing on good product that will lead to a perfect bonbon in the end."
Two-voice canonical. The choreography metaphor + relay-of-care quality model both present in the team's own words. Foundation for Cluster 09 supporting article "How Bonbons Are Made: From Ganache to Glaze." Don't paraphrase Edgar's "starburst" — that's named-technique gold.

The wildflower honey caramel origin (2007)

Bonbon origin · NEW founder story
"The wildflower honey caramel was originally created in my home kitchen in the early days of French Broad Chocolates in 2007. It was an attempt to make a caramel without corn syrup. Knowing that honey is a sugar that has invert sugar properties — like corn syrup, to prevent crystallization — I tried using honey in place of corn syrup. Honey boils at a different temperature than corn syrup, and the flavor — it starts to develop a smoky flavor when you get it to temperature.

"So in making the recipe, it smelled kind of a little smoky, a little bitter, and I thought it was a total fail. I dumped it out on a piece of parchment and moved on with my next project, and then came back to it later and ate a piece. The smoke was actually a really interesting flavor — like, with the floral notes of the honey, the bitterness from the smoke actually cut the sweetness. So where most caramel is cloying, this was super well balanced. And I just kept going back and slicing off pieces and eating it. And that was, I would say, our first signature bonbon."
Previously-uncaptured founder story. The corollary to the truffle-rolling moment, but for the bonbon line. The "total fail → first signature" arc is signature Jael — the universe-conspires register, applied to a single recipe. Use as the narrative spine for Cluster 09 pillar and the on-product-page story for the Wildflower Honey Caramel SKU.
— Jael · confex transcript, FBC confex 1.m4a · 2026-04-27

"What is a Bonbon?" — pillar page lead

Cluster 09 pillar · constructed · confections-team-led
A bonbon is a small chocolate sculpture with a soft filling and a snappy chocolate shell. That's the technical answer. The longer answer is that a bonbon is a playground.

Our Head Chocolatier Edgar calls the production line a choreography. Twenty thousand bonbons leave our Asheville kitchen every week, made by a seven-person team using empty-mold rehearsals, hand-tempered cocoa butter, paintbrushes flicked backwards into a starburst, and the kind of small movements that take a year to make look effortless. Every step has eyes on it from the next person down the line — what our Confections Supervisor Rihanna calls "the standard of excellence at every step."

Inside the shell, the filling is a ganache, a caramel, or both — handmade in batches of around five thousand, monitored for sugar concentration and water activity so the bonbon stays delicious for three months without a single preservative. We make our own dark chocolate from cacao we source ourselves, which means the shell isn't just a wrapper. It's the backbone of the flavor. Balanced, but not subtle, as Edgar says.

What's the difference between a bonbon and a truffle? A truffle is a specific kind of bonbon — soft ganache, hand-rolled, dusted in cocoa powder, named after the mushroom it resembles. A bonbon is the broader category. In America, we tend to call all of it "truffles." We're agnostic about what you call them. We care about what's inside.

The first bonbon we ever made was a wildflower honey caramel, in Jael's home kitchen in 2007. She was trying to make a caramel without corn syrup. The honey smoked when she got it to temperature; she thought it was a total fail and dumped it on a piece of parchment. She came back later, ate a piece, and that became our first signature bonbon. Most caramel is cloying. The smoke cut the sweetness. We've been making bonbons that way since — in the gap between what was supposed to happen and what actually tasted good.

Cross-segment patterns

Four patterns recur across all six audiences. They should anchor any cross-channel messaging.

Pattern 01 · Story-led purchase

Across all six segments, customers are buying with a story, not despite it. Even Segment 4 (the most utilitarian) chooses French Broad Chocolates over commodity bulk because of who and what is behind the bag.

For three segments (DTC, Gift, Walk-in) Asheville is part of the value. For two (Retail B2B, Foodservice) it's a credible-source signal. For one (B2B Cacao) it's incidental.

Pattern 03 · Relationship-led economics

Subscription rather than one-off. Long-term wholesale rather than transactional. Co-import rather than spot-buy. The brand is built for high-LTV / low-CAC dynamics.

Implication: marketing should match — invest in audience depth over reach.

The single emotional through-line across every segment is pleasure-as-vital, not pleasure-as-transgression. From the Tuesday-night ritual to the wholesale buyer choosing "people-pleasing" over "connoisseur-only."

Implication: if a piece of audience-specific copy could read as joyless, it's wrong — regardless of segment.

The architectural pattern — hub vs. pillar

Two of our clusters separate the navigation hub from the content pillar that drives traffic. This becomes the documented pattern, not an exception.

Pattern

Hub-vs-pillar split

The hub (e.g., /learn/pairings/) is a thin navigation page. It exists for site architecture and internal-linking equity. It does not target a high-volume keyword.

The pillar (e.g., /learn/pairings/chocolate-and-wine-pairings/) is the actual content asset that captures organic search traffic. It targets the head term in its cluster.

The hub links to all spokes; the pillar is one of the spokes — but the most ambitious one. Spokes link laterally to other spokes and back up to the hub.

Where it applies

Two clusters use this pattern

Asheville Local SEO. Hub: /asheville/ · Pillar: /asheville/chocolate-guide/ targeting "asheville chocolate" (2,900/mo).

Pairings. Hub: /learn/pairings/ · Pillar: /learn/pairings/chocolate-and-wine-pairings/ targeting "chocolate and wine" (8,100/mo).

All other clusters use the conventional pillar-as-hub model. The hub-vs-pillar split is reserved for clusters where the natural hub keyword has thin volume but the cluster has clear high-volume spoke intent.
