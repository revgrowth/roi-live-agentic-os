# French Broad Chocolates — Positioning

> The competitive position, brand promise, and substantive claims the company can make that no other chocolate brand can.
> Every claim in this file must be source-traceable to founder quotes or verifiable facts. If a claim drifts toward generic craft-chocolate marketing, it goes.

---

## 1. The one-sentence position

**French Broad Chocolates is the bean-to-bar chocolate company that does all of it — sourcing, manufacturing, confections, pastry, retail, and education — and does each one deeply, from a 20-year home in Asheville that we treat as a cultural co-founder.**

This sentence is a synthesis of three founder claims; it is not a constructed slogan:

- *"We do all of it, and we do it deeply."* (Jael, competitive transcript — the dinner-party answer.)
- *"Even though the cacao isn't grown here in Asheville, we believe the terroir of Asheville is influencing our business."* (Jael, origin transcript — the cultural-terroir framing.)
- *"We've been doing this for almost twenty years."* (Repeated; founded 2006.)

## 2. Brand promise

| Pillar | What we promise | What we point to as proof |

## 3. The competitive landscape

The founders frame the landscape in three concentric layers.

### Layer 1 — Industrial chocolate (Hershey, Mars, Cadbury, Lindt, Godiva, Ghirardelli)

This is the comparison customers default to. The honest answer:

> *"Honest answer, you can [get good chocolate at Whole Foods for half the price]. And I mean that. We are not trying to win on price. What we're offering is fundamentally different, not just in how it tastes, but in how it was made and what it's connected to. When you buy French Broad Chocolate, you're paying for the sourcing, the craft, the people, the spaces, and the way we show up in our community. You're paying for everything that made that bar possible. That's a different thing than a bar of chocolate."* — Jael, competitive transcript

Use this position whenever a customer compares price-per-bar.

### Layer 2 — Craft / bean-to-bar peers (Dandelion, Dick Taylor, Fruition, Ritual, and the broader "Class of 2010")

These are kin, not enemies. The founders genuinely do not want to attack peers, and Jael will name this on record:

> *"I don't want to go on record saying anyone is missing the point. But I will, again, just say where we have a different point of view."*

The point of difference is **pleasure-forward craft**:

> *"A lot of craft chocolate got very focused on purity, sometimes at the expense of pleasure. There's a quiet assumption in parts of this industry that more austere equals better, that bitter, darker equals better. But chocolate is emotional. It's joyful. It's fun. It's meant to be eaten and shared and enjoyed without a lecture. We've always believed that great sourcing and great craft should lead to something deeply delicious and accessible — not something that needs explaining before you're allowed to like it."* — Jael, competitive transcript

Use this position when speaking to a chocolate-curious but not connoisseur audience.

### Layer 3 — Adjacent specialty food (specialty coffee, craft beer, single-origin tea, natural wine)

This is where French Broad Chocolates wants to live in the customer's mental map. They cite these adjacencies repeatedly:

> *"There's a part of our industry, craft chocolate, which we feel kinship with craft beer and with specialty coffee, in that we're paying attention to process and to sourcing."* — Jael
> *"Going to the taproom at your local brewery, or sipping on your morning cup of single-origin coffee, or picking up your CSA box from your local farm…"* — Dan

Use this position for educational content, tour scripts, and any time the brand needs to explain *why* origin matters by analogy.

## 4. What only French Broad Chocolates can claim

Each item below is an actual differentiator — a thing that is either factually exclusive to the brand, or a stance/practice that competitors do not credibly hold.

### 4.1 Vertical integration at this scale, in this category

> *"Most chocolate companies pick a lane. We do all of it, and we do it deeply."*

**The claim:** under one roof, French Broad Chocolates does cacao sourcing, chocolate manufacturing, bonbons + confections, baked pastry, brownies, drinking chocolates, ice cream, retail café, e-commerce, wholesale (both finished-goods and B2B-cacao), tours, and education.

**Why it's defensible:** the founders have stress-tested this. Most bean-to-bar makers are lean and product-line-narrow. Most chocolate cafés don't make chocolate. Most chocolate manufacturers don't run cafés. French Broad Chocolates does all of it after almost twenty years and weathered Hurricane Helene because of the diversification.

**The sharpest version of this claim — bean-to-bar to bonbon:** Edgar, Head Chocolatier, in the confex transcript: *"There's not a lot of bean to bar to bonbon places in the world."* The vertical integration goes further than most competitors realize. FBC makes its own dark chocolate — Edgar calls it *"balanced, but not subtle"* — and that house dark becomes the structural backbone of the bonbons (the shell + many of the fillings). Most chocolatiers buy chocolate as an ingredient. FBC makes it. **This is the highest-defensibility version of the "we do all of it" claim.** When the audience is technical or skeptical, lead here.

### 4.2 The experiential factory

> *"Our chocolate factory is big and beautiful by design. Most chocolate factories are utilitarian and closed off and inaccessible. Ours is meant to be walked through, experienced, and shared. That's an investment many companies don't or can't make."* — Jael

**The claim:** the 821 Riverside Drive Chocolate Factory & Café (opened 2018) was deliberately designed for guests, not just for production. Glass separating customers from the production floor. Tours three times a day.

**Why it's defensible:** factories cost more to build like this. The architectural choice itself is a differentiator competitors haven't replicated.

### 4.3 Pleasure-forward craft (an explicit ideological position)

> *"Great sourcing and great craft should lead to something deeply delicious and accessible — not something that needs explaining before you're allowed to like it."*

**The claim:** the brand explicitly resists the "darker = better, more austere = better" arc of craft chocolate. The product line is intentionally diverse: peanut butter cups sit beside single-origin Uganda 70%; brown butter milk chocolate sits beside scorpion pepper 72%.

**Why it's defensible:** This is a stated, repeated values-claim from the founders that lines up with the actual product line. Competitors who sell only single-origin dark cannot make this claim without changing what they sell.

### 4.4 Sourcing relationships measured in decades, not seasons

The Dan-led narrative makes specific claims that other craft makers simply cannot match without similar history:

- 14-year direct buying relationship with Cacao Bisiesto (Nicaragua).
- 19-year buying relationship with Daniel South in Costa Rica until his passing in 2024.
- Active development of a successor relationship in the Bribri indigenous region of Costa Rica.
- Co-import partnerships with peer chocolate makers (Rocka Chocolate in Atlanta, Parliament, 24 Fort Blackbirds in California) — sharing risk on container loads.

**Why it's defensible:** time-in-market and named relationships are the evidence. Cannot be claimed without doing it.

### 4.5 A 20-year, weathered history

The brand has survived dropping out of school, COVID, cacao price tripling ($3K → $12K/ton), and Hurricane Helene. The Helene story specifically is becoming a defining piece of brand mythology:

- Water reached 39 inches up the front door.
- 8 inches of water inside the building.
- Production floor, three feet higher, stayed dry — *the only reason the business survived*.
- Rebuilt 2025 by accelerating online + wholesale; lost ~$1M in retail, nearly recovered through other channels.

**Why it's defensible:** verifiable, recent, and emotionally true. This is a wholesale-conversation differentiator (resilience signal) and a customer-loyalty differentiator (community-led recovery).

### 4.6 Asheville as cultural co-founder

> *"Even though the cacao isn't grown here in Asheville, we believe the terroir of Asheville is influencing our business. This is a place that is creative, that is collaborative."*

**The claim:** the brand is inseparable from Asheville's food and craft community. Local sourcing partnerships (Counter Culture coffee, Sandy Mush strawberries, Annie Louise's pumpkins). Founders on nonprofit boards. Multi-business collaborations (Mother's Day collection with six women-founded local food businesses).

**Why it's defensible:** the integration is real and observable. A competitor in another city cannot make this claim about Asheville. A competitor in Asheville cannot match the depth.

### 4.7 The river is the brand metaphor — and it is actually meaningful

Other chocolate brands name themselves after places. French Broad Chocolates uses its river as a working metaphor for evolution, resilience, and constancy-through-change.

> *"The French Broad River is a metaphor of life and of business — it represents the only real constant in life, which is change."*

The metaphor was made concrete in 2024 when the river the company is named after rose and flooded its building. The metaphor is no longer rhetorical — it is documented history.

## 6. Audience-by-audience positioning lines

The position above adapts. These are the angle and lead-line for each major audience.

### 6.1 DTC chocolate buyer (subscription, e-commerce, direct gifting)

**Angle:** "Chocolate that brings you closer to who you are, not further from it."

**Lead claim:** A bonbon-of-the-month or a single bar arriving at the door is a small ceremony. Twelve months of new flavors, a slow Sunday afternoon, a five-minute pause that matters more than you think.

**Proof:** Hand-crafted bonbons. Twelve new flavors a month in the subscription. Single-origin bars from named farms. Compostable packaging.

### 6.2 Gift purchaser (corporate gifting, special occasions, host gifts)

**Angle:** "A gift that comes with a story."

**Lead claim:** Beautiful boxes, named origins, a card that tells the recipient something they couldn't have told themselves. Asheville on the front door, the world inside.

**Proof:** Curated gift sets. Mother's Day collection co-built with six women-founded Asheville businesses. 20-year track record on hospitality and packaging detail.

### 6.3 B2B wholesale buyer (specialty grocery, gift shops, like-minded shopkeepers)

**Angle:** "We do the hard part of the chocolate story for you. You sell something your customers can ask follow-up questions about and you'll have answers."

**Lead claim:** A chocolate brand with named producers, 20 years of consistency, a manufacturing operation that survived a flood, and a story your floor staff can tell with two sentences of training.

**Proof:** Pillars 1–3 above. Extensive press. Good Food Awards. Jael's Southern Fork interview as a one-page sales pitch. Dan's sourcing transcript as the proof for any buyer who wants depth.

### 6.4 B2B chocolate-as-ingredient (pastry chefs, chocolatiers, food manufacturers)

**Angle:** "Chocolate from a maker that knows what you're doing with it, because we do it too."

**Lead claim:** Bulk chocolate from the same factory that produces our own confections, pastry, and brownies. Direct-sourced cacao with reliable quality specs. We share notes.

**Proof:** Dan's QA program (regular lab analysis, in-house testing). Co-import partnerships with peer makers. Capacity to produce ~500kg dark batches and ~250kg milk batches.

### 6.5 Café / retail walk-in customer (Lounge, Factory)

**Angle:** "A place that feels like home and tastes like a story."

**Lead claim:** Sit down. Order an ice cream, a hot chocolate, a brownie, a flight of bonbons. Stay an hour. Watch people make chocolate through the glass.

**Proof:** The Chocolate Lounge has been at Pack Square for 12 years. The Factory at Riverside opened 2018. Tours three times a day.

### 6.6 Restaurant + hotel partner (the dessert program / amenity sale)

**Angle:** "A local chocolate program that elevates your menu and a partner story you can name on the table tent."

**Lead claim:** Chocolate that sources, makes, and tells the story of itself — and is made by neighbors, not vendors.

**Proof:** 20-year hospitality history. Long-standing local restaurant + chef relationships. Available formats: bulk chocolate, custom bonbons, custom bars, drinking chocolate, ice-cream base.

### 6.7 Press, food media, industry peers

**Angle:** "The bean-to-bar maker that built the experiential factory model and stayed pleasure-forward through 20 years of craft chocolate's purity wave."

**Lead claim:** 50 tons of chocolate a year. 85+ team. A factory designed to be walked through. A founder who was at the original Mendocino chocolate-camp gathering and one who travels the supply chain in person.

**Proof:** Class-of-2010 timeline. Good Food Awards. Helene survival narrative. Dan's industry candor. Jael's nonprofit board service.

## 7. The brand promise hierarchy (what to lead with when)

When a piece of content needs to compress to a single message, use this order:

1. **The pleasure** is non-negotiable. Lead here when the reader is a consumer.
2. **The integrity of source** is non-negotiable. Lead here when the reader is a buyer who cares about supply chain.
3. **The craft of the make** is non-negotiable. Lead here when the reader is technical (other makers, chefs).
4. **The Asheville home** is the differentiating context. Lead here when the reader is regionally tied or local.
5. **The 20 years** is the credibility frame. Lead here when the reader is comparing French Broad Chocolates to a newer brand.
6. **The all-of-it scope** is the mode of operation. Lead here when the reader is comparing to a single-line bean-to-bar maker.

Never lead with luxury, premium, decadence, or any word that contradicts §1.1 of `voice-profile.md`.

## 8. Risks to the position (things to watch)

These are the open vulnerabilities the founders have already named. The positioning content should be honest about each.

- **Cacao price volatility.** Tripling in 2024 changed unit economics; the brand's price-relative-to-Lindt argument has to be made carefully.
- **Asheville-tourism dependence.** Helene exposed it. Diversification into online + wholesale is a strategic priority through 2026, not a marketing claim.
- **Sourcing reshuffles.** Daniel South's death, the Costa Rica successor in development, Uganda as a newer origin not yet in the founder transcripts. The named-relationships strength has to be kept current.
- **Confections + pastry team voice not yet captured.** The "all of it, deeply" claim depends on those teams being credibly visible. They are not yet in the brand-context corpus.
- **Heavy-metals (cadmium, lead) regulatory pressure.** Dan covers this candidly in his interview. It is a current risk; the brand should be ready to talk about it.

### Bonbon vs. truffle — terminology note for marketing copy

Edgar (Head Chocolatier) gives the technical answer in the confex transcript: a truffle is a hand-rolled, cocoa-dusted soft ganache in a thin chocolate shell — *"a truffle is a bonbon, but a bonbon isn't necessarily a truffle."* But he also acknowledges: *"in America, we kind of more generally refer to that category, that bonbon category, as truffles. So we've had a lot of conversation about this and what not only what is most technically true… it's also what best communicates to the customer. It's an active and live conversation."*

**Implication for copy:** treat this as a documented brand-internal-debate, not a settled position. Cluster 09's *"Bonbon vs Truffle vs Praline"* article should preserve the nuance — explain the technical distinction, then acknowledge the pragmatic American convention. Don't assert a single right answer.

## 10. Voice fit

This position must sound like the voice profile. When in doubt:

- Open with a scene (Jael move) or a corrected simplification (Dan move).
- Name a specific person, place, or quantity within the first paragraph.
- Avoid "premium," "luxury," "decadent," "indulgent," "guilt-free," "FBC."
- Map back to "Sourced with integrity / Crafted with intention / Served with gratitude" if the piece is brand-level.
- End with a community gesture if the piece is consumer-facing.
