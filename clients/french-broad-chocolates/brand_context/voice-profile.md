# French Broad Chocolates — Voice Profile

> A multi-stakeholder voice profile. Three load-bearing voices (Jael · Dan · Confections team) share a brand-level core. Other team voices (pastry, hospitality, tour guides, chocolate-making) will layer in as interviews land. Treat this as a living document; cite source quotes when adding to it.

---

## Section 1 — The Shared Brand Voice

What shows up consistently in Jael, Dan, and the published work — the load-bearing patterns that make a piece of writing read as French Broad Chocolates regardless of which founder voiced it.

### 1.1 The animating belief

> "Chocolate is a food just like grain is a food. One of the principal purposes of food is pleasure."
> — Jael, *The Southern Fork*

> "Knowing where something comes from changes how you experience it."
> — Jael, repeated across the competitive transcript and origin recordings

Everything French Broad Chocolates writes downstreams from these two ideas, in this order:

1. **Chocolate is a food.** Not a luxury, not a guilty pleasure, not a sweet. A food shaped by people, place, and process — like wine, coffee, or bread.
2. **Pleasure is the point.** Source seriously, craft seriously, then make something deeply delicious. Never make pleasure the prerequisite for understanding.

If a draft doesn't honor both — if it tilts toward purity-as-superiority OR cheapens chocolate into "indulgence" / "decadence" / "guilt-free" — it's off-brand.

### 1.2 Specificity over abstraction

The voice is **always specific**. Names, places, numbers, dates, processes.

- Jael: "We source the finest cacao from farmers and producers in Costa Rica, Guatemala, Nicaragua, and Peru, and transform it into 50 tons of chocolate a year."
- Dan: "8 inches of water inside the building" / "39 inches up our front door" / "12 and a half metric ton container" / "$7 to $10 per kilogram" / "12 hours of conching"
- Jael: "Cooperativa Norandino" / "Daniel South" / "Cacao Bisiesto" / "Cacao Zorzal"

**Rule:** When tempted to write "our farmers" or "our partners," stop and name them. When tempted to write "high-quality cacao," stop and explain what the quality metric is.

### 1.3 People, place, and process — as a structural device

This is the founders' three-part lens. They use it spontaneously and often.

> "It is not only a plant, it is people, and it is place."
> — Jael, *The Southern Fork*

> "We treat cacao as the agricultural product that it is, which means it's variable by harvest, by weather, by the hands that fermented it."
> — Dan, on craft chocolate process

Use this structure when explaining anything about cacao, chocolate, or the company's choices. It's a thinking tool, not just a slogan.

### 1.4 Story-rooted claims

A claim never floats. It's grounded in a moment, a person, or a place:

- The river didn't flood the factory in the abstract; the river "rose, and rose and rose and breached her banks and crossed the street, onto our side of the road, climbed the hill, climbed the steps to our front door."
- Sustainability isn't a value statement; it's a 39-inch waterline and a production floor sitting three feet higher.
- "Connection to source" isn't a slogan; it's Jael moving "to another country and leaving behind all friends, family, houses, cars, things" four months pregnant.

**Rule:** Before writing an abstract claim, find the concrete scene that proves it.

### 1.5 Anti-pretension

Both founders actively dismantle expertise-as-gatekeeping.

- Jael: "I will never turn up my nose at a milk chocolate bar, or a creamy peanut butter cup, or what I will go to my grave believing is the best dessert in the world, a chocolate chip cookie."
- Dan: "I don't profess to understand all of the variables."
- Jael: "Hilarious beginner's luck."
- Dan: "I don't actually know what our cost per ounce is. That cost… does vary, quite quite a lot."

**Rule:** When craft-chocolate language threatens to become a credentialing exercise, undercut it. Admit what you don't know. Name the simpler pleasure.

### 1.6 Asheville as a character, not a backdrop

Asheville isn't where the company is located. It's how the company exists.

> "Even though the cacao isn't grown here in Asheville, we believe the terroir of Asheville is influencing our business."
> — Jael, origin transcript

> "Asheville gave us everything. Being an active part of its growth and resilience is core to who we are."
> — Jael, competitive transcript

The river is the central metaphor, but the practical anchor is the community: named local suppliers (Sandy Mush strawberries, Annie Louise's pumpkins, Counter Culture coffee), named local institutions (Mountain BizWorks, Asheville Savings Bank, Habitat home store, Grove Park Inn), named neighbors. Use them.

### 1.7 We do all of it, and we do it deeply

The single most repeated competitive line. It is the clearest brand promise.

> "Most chocolate companies pick a lane. We do all of it, and we do it deeply. We source cacao with integrity. We make many different varieties of chocolate from scratch with care, and we turn it into bars, bonbons, brownies, pastries, and drinking chocolates. And then, we invite people into our spaces to experience the whole story."
> — Jael, competitive transcript

Note the rhythm: a flat declarative ("We do all of it"), a refrain ("and we do it deeply"), then a list of acts (source / make / turn into / invite). This four-beat pattern recurs — use it when articulating what the company does.

### 1.8 Three-pillar published framework (live on the site)

The website crystalizes the founders' values into a tighter triplet that is genuinely brand-level — not from the original voice guide, but already in production:

> "We make awesome chocolate — sourced with integrity, crafted with intention, and served with gratitude."

| Pillar | What it covers |
|---|---|
| **Sourced with integrity** | Cacao + ingredient origins. Direct trade, named producers, above-commodity pricing, agroforestry, transparency. |
| **Crafted with intention** | Manufacturing process, recipe development, quality choices, the 12-step bean-to-bar workflow. |
| **Served with gratitude** | Hospitality, retail experience, tours, community contribution, team culture. |

This is the strongest unifying skeleton currently in production. New copy should map to one (or transparently bridge two) of these three.

### 1.9 Universal sensory verbs and frames

Words that belong to French Broad Chocolates and recur across both founders + the site:

- **"Deeply"** — the most overused single word, but earned. Deeply delicious. Deep relationships. Deep web of connection. Deep tasting experiences. Use when intensifying — but not as a filler adverb.
- **"Connection to source"** — preferred over "farm to table" or "transparency."
- **"People, place, and process"** — see §1.3.
- **"Heart-centered"** — Jael, on the Costa Rica café experience. Used sparingly but on-brand.
- **"Edible jewels" / "small sculptures"** — Jael's preferred frames for bonbons.
- **"The whole story"** — what they invite people into.
- **"Joy, connection, offering"** — Jael's three-beat description of what food does.
- **"Taste with intention"** — directive used across content.

## Section 2 — Jael's Voice Signature

Jael is the brand's primary public-facing voice. She does the storytelling, the founder narrative, the customer-emotional register. She is the voice that talks to Mother's Day shoppers, podcast listeners, the wholesale buyer who needs to feel a story, and the journalist writing a profile.

### 2.1 Five identifying patterns

**(a) Refrain-builds.** She has a handful of phrases she returns to like a chorus, and she lets them swing into the rhythm of the sentence. They aren't slogans — they're the way she thinks out loud.

> "We do all of it, and we do it deeply."
> "Joy, connection, offering."
> "People, place, and process."
> "I want it to bring me closer to who I am."
> "We are better together."

**(b) Three-beat phrasing.** She frequently lands on lists of three, and the three are usually feelings or actions, not nouns.

> "Beautiful and messy; frustrating and deeply fulfilling; unpredictable and humbling."
> "It's a place for education, for community, for storytelling, and for transparency." *(four — but the rhythm is the point)*
> "Source cacao with integrity. Make chocolate from scratch with care. Turn it into something beautiful."

**(c) Reframing — from absence to presence.** When she lands on something hard or constraining, she pivots to what she wants instead.

> "I think that it's an example of looking at life through the lens of things that you want, not things that you don't want. So what I want is, is a meaningful experience with food that is flavorful and pleasureful, and I don't want to approach food as something that takes me away from who I am. I want it to bring me closer to who I am."
> — *The Southern Fork*

> "I'm interested in shifting that focus from scarcity to abundance."
> — On what keeps her up at night

This is signature. Jael never lectures, but she does redirect.

**(d) Universe-conspires / serendipity register.** Jael frames consequential decisions as recognized signs, not strategic plans. This is real to her, not affected.

> "We felt that kind of familiar serendipity of, like, this was a clue that needed to be heard, that needed to be born."
> — On choosing Asheville

> "A la *The Alchemist*… when you decide something, the world conspires, or the universe conspires to make it happen."

> "Chocolate found me."

**(e) Self-disclosure that closes a loop into product or invitation.** Her published writing tells a personal story, then folds the brand back in without a hard sell.

> *(after a long honest piece on motherhood)* "If you think chocolate might help (it certainly can't hurt), maybe you want to try a limited edition cocktail collection? Or my personal favorite, a box of peanut butter tumblers? They always make it better for me. A couple pieces in, you might realize, whatever your messy and beautiful situation, that everything is going to be ok."
> — Jael's "Being a Mom" journal post

Notice: parenthetical levity ("it certainly can't hurt"), her own preference named ("my personal favorite, a box of peanut butter tumblers"), and the closing reframe back to the original emotion ("messy and beautiful situation… everything is going to be ok").

### 2.2 Vocabulary tells

Words and constructions that flag a Jael draft:

- "Hi friends," / "Hi all," / "you know what?"
- "Y'all" (sparingly, after Asheville assimilation)
- "Honestly," / "truly," / "really"
- "Gosh," / "oh man,"
- "Hilarious" applied to past struggle ("hilarious beginner's luck")
- "Heart-centered," "deep-meaningful," "lifelong learners"
- "Storge" — Greek love-types reference (real, not borrowed — she found it from a Chicago Uber driver and uses it earnestly)
- Slash-stack signoff: "Co-founder/CEO/Mom/Human" — she lists multiple identities at once

### 2.3 Sentence rhythm

Jael's spoken voice is loose, conversational, and runs in long arcs. Her written voice (e.g., the Mother's Day post) tightens the same arcs into shorter declarative beats. **In writing, she doesn't transcribe herself — she compresses.**

Her sentences often build via accumulation:

> "We were feeling free and spontaneous and romantic, and we had a great trip."

She uses the polysyndeton ("and… and… and…") to mimic memory and momentum, especially in origin storytelling. Use it sparingly and on purpose.

### 2.4 What Jael avoids

- Lecture mode. She does not explain unless invited.
- Numbers as the primary point. (She'll mention 85 employees, 50 tons, but the emotional point lands first.)
- Critique by name. ("I don't want to go on record saying anyone is missing the point.") She redirects: "I will, again, just say where we have a different point of view."
- The word "luxury" as the headline frame. She'll let an interviewer use it and gently push back.

## Section 3 — Dan's Voice Signature

Dan is the brand's process-deep voice. He does sourcing, manufacturing, ethics, and the unglamorous truth of supply chains. He is the voice that talks to a wholesale buyer asking real questions, an industry peer, a journalist writing about cacao economics, or a consumer who wants the unsanitized version.

### 3.1 Five identifying patterns

**(a) Anti-reductionism as a stance.** This is the most distinctive thing about Dan. He is openly suspicious of marketing simplification — including his own brand's prior simplifications — and he says so.

> "I've been using the term direct trade a lot less lately because I've been growing allergic to reductionist messages because I don't find them to be very intellectually honest."

> "Direct trade is a very convenient marketing term, but it is probably impossibly reductionist because cacao necessarily passes through many hands."

> "I don't want to oversimplify things because… infinite complexity surrounds us."

> "It is far more complex than that."

**Rule for Dan-voice copy:** if a sentence sounds tidy, interrogate it before you ship it. Add the qualification.

**(b) Real-time self-correction.** He'll say something, hear it as he says it, and adjust.

> "We try and we've been working toward getting on a cycle of purchasing with our most deepest partnerships, so that we can have the greatest control possible. No, not that. Let me strike that. It's not to have the greatest control possible. It's to have the greatest reliable partnership possible."

This is real. In edited writing, the equivalent is: lead with the conventional framing, then explicitly correct it. ("It's not X — it's actually Y.") It's a load-bearing rhetorical move, not a tic to clean up.

**(c) Specific names + specific quantities, dense.**

> "$7 to $10 per kilogram. So that is quite different from dollars per pound. So $7 is $3.18 per pound."
> "12 and a half metric ton container."
> "Up to $15 a kilogram at the height of it."
> "500 kilograms… approximately 12 hours of conching… 0.5% lecithin by weight."

His writing carries the weight of someone who has done the math and knows the freight cost, the broker fee, the shrinkage rate, and the moisture spec by heart.

**(d) Tender humanism inside technical narrative.** Dan's softest moments are inside his most technical answers.

> "I don't know that I have ever visited a coa farm that wasn't proud of the cocoa they produced. There is a lot of pride and a lot of intense passion for producing cocoa."

> *(on Daniel South, his Costa Rica supplier of 19 years)* "He was 63 when he died… he will be missed. It's a big loss for that region… growing cocoa in that area is challenging and producing high quality fermented dried cocoa is even more challenging, but he did a great job and I will always miss him."

This is signature Dan: a technical paragraph with one human sentence in it, untreated, unembellished. Don't sand them off.

**(e) Big-picture leveling.** Dan repeatedly puts French Broad Chocolates *inside* the system rather than above it.

> "We are not separate from commodity. We are all connected. The geopolitics of the cocoa industry, the strategic decisions of big candy and big chocolate makers, the people that are majority producers of chocolate in the world. All those dynamics, they all affect us."

> "We started out this journey in chocolate assuming that what we were doing was, you know, really unique, and we've come to find that most everything has been done before, and that's beautiful."

> "Craft chocolate is not new. As long as chocolate has been around, people have been crafting it."

This is the antidote to craft-chocolate exceptionalism. Use it when the brand voice is in danger of sounding self-congratulatory.

### 3.2 Vocabulary tells

Words and constructions that flag a Dan draft:

- "Reductionist" / "intellectually honest" / "myriad" / "thorny"
- "I don't want to oversimplify things"
- "Generally speaking"
- "To a degree" / "to a certain extent"
- "It is what it is" used non-defensively
- Specific cacao-trade vocabulary: agroforestry, fermentation, post-harvest, percentage-fermentation, Hispaniola/red cocoa, conching, winnowing, cocoa butter, lecithin, particle dispersion, volatilization
- "I don't actually know" / "I don't know the answer to that" — said calmly
- Editorial framing: "Speaking broadly," / "in any case," / "in the end,"
- Whimsical asides about mechanism: "It would be a wild journey" (re: a GoPro on a cocoa bean); "Lecithin is pretty pretty magical"

### 3.3 Sentence rhythm

Dan's sentences run long. He stacks subordinate clauses. He'll begin with a hedge, layer in a qualification, give a specific example, then circle back to a quiet definitive. **In writing, the rhythm should preserve the layering — don't chop his thinking into staccato.**

Example pattern: claim → "but" → counter-context → "however" → tighter claim.

> "Craft chocolate makers have always paid above commodity prices, not as charity, but because quality cacao comes from skilled farmers and fermenters who deserve to be paid for their expertise. It's a business partnership that works for everyone. That's the ideal. Not saying that we have globally reached that, but that is the vision."

### 3.4 What Dan avoids

- Slogans.
- "Premium" as a claim.
- Anything that sounds like greenwashing — he'll name it as such. (On certifications: "It sounds like… you might call it greenwashing.")
- Single-sentence answers when the question is complex.
- Putting French Broad Chocolates above the industry.

## Section 4 — Team Voices

The brand voice is anchored by Jael and Dan, but it isn't just two voices. Multiple teams contribute to public-facing FBC content — and as interview audio lands, each team gets its own §4.x sibling here, structured the same way as §2 and §3 (five identifying patterns · vocabulary tells · sentence rhythm · what they avoid).

Pre-allocated placeholders below mark which teams are still pending. Don't move team voices into §1 (shared brand voice) until at least one full interview is captured — §1 is reserved for patterns provably common across two or more stakeholders.

### 4.0 Why team voices matter

Three reasons:

1. **Authority depth.** A page on bonbon-making sounds different — and ranks differently — when it's voiced by the Head Chocolatier than when it's a Jael-voice marketing summary. Search engines and AI assistants increasingly reward content with named, role-specific expertise.
2. **Brand integrity.** When customers visit the Lounge, take a tour, or write a wholesale email, they're talking to the team, not the founders. The published voice should match the in-person experience.
3. **Vertical-integration credibility.** "We do all of it, and we do it deeply" is undermined if the website only sounds like two people. The cluster strategy explicitly names team voices as information-gain assets (see `projects/onboarding/cluster-expansion-opportunities.md`).

### 4.1 Confections team — Rihanna Bourbon + Edgar

Captured in `inputs/2026-04-27_confex-team-transcripts.md`. Two voices, one shared register. Rihanna Bourbon is the Confections Supervisor (transcript renders title as *"Perfection supervisor"* — almost certainly transcription artifact; flagged H08 in open-gaps). Edgar is Head Chocolatier (last name pending — also H08). Edgar publicly calls Rihanna *"the keeper of excellence"* — a phrase worth repeating.

#### Five identifying patterns

**(a) Process-as-choreography.** Edgar coined this. The bonbon production line is described as a series of small movements — empty-mold scraping rehearsals for new staff, the angle of the chocolate scraper, the way you hold the mold. Use this frame whenever describing how the work happens.

> "What we've had to do is establish almost a choreography for new people. We give them an empty mold. Before they even touch the chocolate, we just have them scrape empty molds so that they can at least get used to the movements and sort of have something to memorize and fall back on."
> — Edgar

**(b) Relay-of-care quality model.** Both voices land here. Quality control isn't a station at the end of the line — every team member checks the previous step's work before moving forward.

> "Each person has to meet that standard of excellence at their steps so that they're passing on good product that will lead to a perfect bonbon in the end."
> — Rihanna

> "If somebody on the packaging team, they can't see how thick that cap is. They're just looking at the outside of the bonbon."
> — Rihanna, on why the relay matters

**(c) Technical with self-deprecating qualifier.** Both speakers go technical fast — Brix, water activity, viscosity, hairline fractures, Maillard reaction — and then catch themselves and offer to translate.

> "I mean, that was very a lot of technical explanation for what we do. And this, you know, being our consumer website, feel free to add anything if it makes it…"
> — Rihanna

> "Can I say a nerdy thing?"
> — Edgar (asking permission before going into yield math)

This is signature. The team owns the science but doesn't lecture. When confections-voice copy reaches a technical density, it should pause and offer the consumer a translation — not strip the technical out.

**(d) Sensory flavor-as-memory.** Edgar's strongest emotional register. Flavors are designed to evoke specific places, times, memories. Nostalgia is a stated craft target.

> "I want them to really feel it in their bones."
> "Nostalgia is one of my favorite feelings to evoke in chocolate."
> — Edgar

The Mother's Day lemon bar bonbon is the canonical example: Edgar's team browned the butter for crust flavor, added cinnamon for graham-cracker association, and a customer reported back that *"it just immediately took me back."*

**(e) Craft-as-team-sport.** Both speakers undermine any single-author narrative. Even when describing their personal contributions, they push credit outward.

> "It shows how much more of a team game this is and how we all contribute our experience and our creativity."
> — Jael, in the same transcript, framing the team correctly

> "We're all kind of doing quality control at every step of the way. Not just one person, but the whole team has to be able to have eyes on everything."
> — Rihanna

#### Rihanna's vocabulary tells

*Imperative · standard of excellence · keeper of excellence (Edgar's phrase for her, which she accepts) · making sure that · paying attention to · 98 out of 100 isn't enough · Brix · water activity · viscosity · moisture monitoring · choreography (echoing Edgar) · perfect.* Rihanna talks in process steps and tolerances; she names the spec before she names the result.

#### Edgar's vocabulary tells

*Playground · choreography · main character energy · in conversation with · hit someone on the heart · feel it in their bones · balance, but not subtle · deep umami bomb · nostalgia · noisette / brown butter as hazelnut · take you on a little ride.* Edgar moves between technical and emotional registers in the same paragraph. He uses theatrical/artistic frames more than process-control frames.

#### Sentence rhythm

Rihanna stacks process steps with technical specs, often building a four-or-five-step micro-sequence inside one paragraph. Her cadence is sequential: *"We start off with… and then we… and then…"* Edgar's rhythm is more lateral — he'll start technical, swing to a sensory anchor, return to a small constraint or trade-off, then close on the human moment.

In writing, preserve both: technical sequences from Rihanna should land as numbered or beat-by-beat process; Edgar's voice should keep the swing — don't flatten his asides into bullet points.

#### What they avoid

- **Luxury / premium / decadent / indulgent** — they don't reach for these words. When they describe what they make, they reach for *art · craft · sculpture · piece · ride · journey · experience.*
- **Lecturing.** Even when the answer is technical, both voices offer a translation or a self-deprecating qualifier ("nerdy thing," "feel free to add anything if it makes it").
- **Purity-as-superiority.** They contrast their work to mass-produced bonbons (Godiva, See's, Lindt) — but the contrast is always specific (*"vegetable oils, flours, stabilizers, emulsifiers, preservatives"*), never moralizing.
- **Single-author claims.** Even when describing personal favorites or signature creations, they push credit to the team or to history.

#### Canonical confections-team paragraphs (use as voice calibration)

## Section 5 — Voice Routing: Who Leads Which Audience

Both founders share the brand voice; their signatures pull in different directions. Use the table to decide whose voice should carry a piece of content (or whose voice should lead while the other supports).

| Audience / Context | Lead voice | Why | Supporting voice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Founder origin storytelling, brand narrative, "why we exist" | **Jael** | Story-driven, scene-rich, emotional. She holds the founder mythology. | Dan can extend with a process detail or a moment from sourcing. |
| Mother's Day / gift-buyer / consumer emotional | **Jael** | Warmth, self-disclosure, reframing. The "Hi friends" register. | — |
| Sustainability / sourcing / direct trade content | **Dan** | He has the lived knowledge and the anti-reductionist credibility. | Jael can frame the "why this matters to us" opener. |
| B2B wholesale, especially other manufacturers + chefs | **Dan** | Technical buyers want technical answers. Dan respects their time. | Jael can frame the values fit. |
| Retail wholesale (shopkeepers, gift-store buyers) | **Either; Jael leads on relationship, Dan leads on product specs** | The pitch is values + product. | — |
| Restaurant + hotel partners | **Jael** | Hospitality-to-hospitality; this is a "values fit" sale. | Dan handles the supply reliability conversation. |
| Press / media (food, business, regional) | **Either, by topic.** Profile = Jael. Industry analysis = Dan. | — | — |
| Crisis / Helene-anniversary / community moments | **Jael** | She holds gratitude. | Dan can name the practical lessons. |
| Manufacturing partners / industry peers (other chocolate makers) | **Dan** | Peer-to-peer, no marketing. | — |
| Tour script / on-site educational | **Either; voice is unified here.** Live audience = both register at once. | — | — |
| Café / lounge experience copy | **Jael** | Service-as-belonging is her register. | — |
| Product description (bonbons, bars, treats) | **Jael** | Sensory + flavor lead. | Dan supports on the sourcing line. |
| Process / how-it's-made content | **Dan** | He owns the workflow. | Jael narrates the why. |
| **Bonbon / confection production** content (Cluster 09) | **Confections team (Edgar + Rihanna)** | They own the work. Edgar leads on flavor/craft; Rihanna leads on process/standards. | Jael frames the consumer "why." |
| **Bonbon flavor development** stories | **Edgar** | Sensory-flavor-as-memory is his register; he holds the development narrative. | Rihanna on standards. Jael frames the brand context. |
| **Standards / quality / training** language | **Rihanna** | "Keeper of excellence." She runs the morning meetings and owns the training language. | — |

**Default routing rule:** If you can't decide, ask which question the piece is answering. *Why does this exist?* → Jael. *How does this work?* → Dan. *Why does it taste like this?* → Both, in that order: Jael opens, Dan deepens. *How does this specific bonbon come to be?* → Confections team leads, Jael closes.

## Section 6 — Universal Rules

### 5.1 Banned phrases and constructions

These are non-negotiable. They contradict the founders' direct quotes.

| Banned | Why | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **"FBC"** in any external copy | Internal shorthand only. | Jael, opening of the competitive transcript: "Let's first make sure that we never call French broad chocolates FBC on our website. That is an internal language thing, and I don't want it to show up in any marketing copy ever." |
| **"Decadent" / "sinful" / "indulgent"** as primary descriptors | Treats chocolate as transgression. Contradicts the food-not-luxury stance. | Jael: "We need chocolate no less than we need grain." |
| **"Guilt-free"** | "There's nothing to feel guilty about." | Brand Voice Guide v1, validated by Jael on *The Southern Fork*. |
| **"Luxury"** — banned as a bare descriptor or marketing headline frame ("luxury chocolate," "luxury experience"). **Permitted** when founders/team use it in qualified phrases that subordinate the word to a specific moment, product, or sensory frame. | The founders explicitly resist the bare/headline use; Jael uses it once in a qualified phrase. | **Banned use:** *"Experience the luxury of French Broad Chocolates."* **Permitted use** (Jael, confex transcript closing line): *"A bonbon is about quality, not quantity… it's a beautiful, small luxury in a moment that transports you."* The qualifier ("small," "in a moment that transports you") subordinates the word — that's the threshold. |
| **"Premium"** as a standalone claim | Unsubstantiated. Show, don't tell. | Brand Voice Guide v1. |
| **"Artisanal"** as a vague descriptor | Use the specific craft instead. | Brand Voice Guide v1. |
| **"Farm to table"** | Overused. Use "connection to source" or "bean to bar." | Brand Voice Guide v1. |
| **"Direct trade"** without nuance | Dan now resists this — he calls it "impossibly reductionist." Use it carefully and qualify it. | Dan, brand voice section. |
| **"Synergy / leverage / disrupt"** and corporate jargon | Off-register. | Brand Voice Guide v1. |
| **Generalizations like "our farmers" without naming** | Voice rule §1.2. | Inferred from both founders' specificity. |

### 5.2 Must-include patterns

When writing from scratch, the piece should hit at least one of these:

- A **named person, place, or supplier**. ("Cacao Bisiesto in Matagalpa." "Daniel South in Puerto Viejo." "Counter Culture coffee, two blocks over.")
- A **scene or moment**, not an abstract claim.
- A **sensory verb or feeling-word** rooted in eating, not in marketing. (Melts, blooms, lingers, snaps, releases, opens.)
- A **mention of community or belonging** when the piece is consumer-facing.
- A **qualification or honest limit** when the piece is sourcing/sustainability — to keep it from sounding like greenwash.

### 5.3 Structural patterns

**(a) The four-beat brand stanza.** Source / make / transform / share. This is the spine of the brand promise. Most "what we do" copy should map to this rhythm:

1. We **source** cacao with integrity.
2. We **make** chocolate from scratch with care.
3. We **transform** it into bars, bonbons, brownies, pastries, and drinking chocolates.
4. We **invite** people into our spaces to experience the whole story.

**(b) Hard fact → soft reframe.** A signature Jael move. Lead with the hard reality, end on the human reframe.

> "Cash is real. Employee payroll is real… Responsibility is something that I hold very seriously to be the steward of this ecosystem… It's a lot."

**(c) Tidy frame → "actually it's more complex" → quiet definitive.** A signature Dan move. Lead with the conventional framing, dismantle it, then restate what the brand actually does.

> *(on direct trade)* "Direct trade is a very convenient marketing term, but it is probably impossibly reductionist because cacao necessarily passes through many hands… But… direct trade with French broad chocolate means we try to know the nuance of those stories of origin."

**(d) Three-pillar mapping.** When the piece is brand-level, map to *Sourced with integrity / Crafted with intention / Served with gratitude* — explicitly or via subheaders. (See §1.8.)

### 5.4 Naming conventions

- **"French Broad Chocolates"** on first reference. "French Broad" acceptable on subsequent references. Never "FBC" externally.
- Capitalize **Chocolate Lounge** and **Chocolate Factory** when they refer to the specific Asheville locations.
- **The river** is "the French Broad" or "the French Broad River" — and is referenced with "she" / "her" both founders use this ("she rose, and rose and rose"; "she generally stays where she belongs"). Keep the river feminine when invoking the metaphor.
- **Cacao** for the agricultural/raw material. **Chocolate** for the finished product. **Cocoa** when talking commodity-trade (coexists with cacao in Dan's voice; both are correct in his usage but "cacao" is the brand-level preference.)
- **"Bean-to-bar"** is hyphenated as adjective, "bean to bar" as noun phrase.

### 6.1 Unified brand voice — short consumer paragraph (constructed)

> Chocolate is a food. Not a luxury, not a decadence, not a guilty thing — a food, shaped by people, place, and process, just like wine or coffee or bread. We source ours from named farmers in Nicaragua, the Dominican Republic, and Uganda. We make it in our factory on Riverside Drive, three feet up from a river that has flooded us once and could again. *Patterns used: §1.1 animating belief; §1.2 specificity (named origins, named street); §1.4 story-rooted (the flood); §5.3a four-beat stanza.*

### 6.2 Jael voice — gift / consumer emotional (constructed, modeled on the Mother's Day blog post)

> Hi friends. Real talk: gifting is hard. Sometimes you want to send the perfect thing and you find yourself frozen, scrolling, second-guessing, unsure if a box of chocolate is enough. Here's what I've learned, after almost twenty years of putting bonbons into other people's hands. The chocolate isn't the gift. The pause is. The slowing down. The two seconds after they open the box and before they take a bite, where they realize someone was thinking of them carefully enough to choose this. Whatever your messy and beautiful situation, that pause matters more than you think. Big love.

*Patterns used: §2.1a refrain ("real talk"); §2.1b three-beat ("frozen, scrolling, second-guessing"); §2.1c reframing (the chocolate is/isn't the gift); §2.1e self-disclosure that loops back to product; §2.2 vocabulary ("real talk," "y'all" register, "big love").*

### 6.3 Jael voice — origin / brand storytelling (verbatim from source)

> "So one day, I'm making truffles by hand, little ganache balls that you roll by hand and then dip in chocolate by hand and then roll in cocoa powder. They were messy, and I had chocolate up to my elbows and complete joy. So this is kind of a meditative act to roll truffles, and I stopped when I felt a tingling in my hands, literally. > — Jael, FBC origin story Q&A

*Why this is gold: scene-first, sensory, builds toward a single quoted line. The whole brand origin is in 100 words. Use it as the canonical telling — every other version is a compression.*

### 6.4 Dan voice — sourcing / sustainability (constructed, modeled on the New Note transcript)

> "Direct trade" is a phrase I've grown allergic to. Cacao necessarily passes through many hands — the people who tend the trees, the people who ferment and dry the beans, the truck drivers, the brokers, the warehouse workers in the East Bay. Calling that *direct* is convenient, and it is probably impossibly reductionist. What we actually try to do is know the nuance of those stories. We've been buying from Cacao Bisiesto in Matagalpa, Nicaragua for fourteen years. We bought from Daniel South in Puerto Viejo, Costa Rica for nineteen, until he passed away last year — at sixty-three, suddenly. We're working now with growers in the Bribri region nearby, helping them get a new operation off the ground. None of this is direct, exactly. It's the long game.

*Patterns used: §3.1a anti-reductionism; §3.1c specific names + dates + numbers; §3.1d tender humanism inside technical narrative; §3.3 layered sentence rhythm; §3.1e long-game framing.*

### 6.5 Dan voice — process / manufacturing (verbatim from source)

> "We treat cacao as the agricultural product that it is, which means it's variable by harvest, by weather, by the hands that fermented it. We develop processes in our chocolate factory built on human discernment, on tradition, and expertise. Rather than trying to make everything taste the same, we have the goal of preserving what's already in the cacao bean rather than processing that out of it."
> — Dan, competitive transcript

*Why this is gold: states the difference in technical terms, then names the value choice (preservation over engineering). Use as the canonical manufacturing-philosophy paragraph.*

### 6.6 Unified — competitive / B2B-leaning (verbatim from source)

> "Most chocolate companies pick a lane. We do all of it, and we do it deeply. We source cacao with integrity. We make many different varieties of chocolate from scratch with care, and we turn it into bars, bonbons, brownies, pastries, and drinking chocolates. And then, we invite people into our spaces to experience the whole story. It's not just a product and a nice label. It's a commitment to relationships with the farmers who grew the cacao, with people on our team who made it, and with the chocolate-loving community we've been a part of for over twenty years."
> — Jael, competitive transcript

*Why this is gold: §5.3a in pure form. This is the canonical competitive/wholesale answer. Every B2B page should descend from this.*

### 6.7 Unified — crisis / community moment (constructed, modeled on Hurricane Helene material)

> The water reached thirty-nine inches up our front door. Eight inches inside. The production floor — three feet higher — stayed dry, and that is the only reason we are still here. What happened next was that our community showed up online and bought every piece of inventory we had, then bought gift cards we couldn't yet redeem, then bought subscriptions for chocolate we couldn't yet make. Our pastry chefs packed boxes. Our baristas printed shipping labels. We learned, the hard way and the right way, what we already suspected: we are stronger together. Asheville gave us everything. Being an active part of its growth and resilience is core to who we are.

*Patterns used: §1.2 specificity (39 inches, 8 inches, 3 feet); §1.4 story-rooted; §1.6 Asheville as character; §5.3b hard fact → soft reframe; closes with a verbatim Jael line.*

## Section 8 — Voice Tests

Apply these before publishing.

1. **The substitution test.** Replace "French Broad Chocolates" with "[any other artisan chocolate brand]." Does the piece still read as accurate? If yes, it's too generic — add a specific name, place, or process detail.
2. **The Jael read.** Would Jael read this and say "yes, that's how we sound" — or would she gently soften something?
3. **The Dan read.** Would Dan find a sentence in here that he'd want to add a "but actually" to? If so, do it for him before publishing.
4. **The lecture test.** Does the piece make a customer feel like they need to study before they're allowed to enjoy? If yes, cut the lecture and lead with the pleasure.
5. **The community test.** If the piece is consumer-facing and longer than a sentence, does it gesture to community, place, or relationship somewhere? If not, find the natural moment to include it.
6. **The honesty test (sourcing/sustainability copy).** Have you qualified the claim? Have you named what's still imperfect? If not, Dan would ask you to.
