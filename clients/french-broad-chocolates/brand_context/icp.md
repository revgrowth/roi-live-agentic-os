# French Broad Chocolates — ICP (Ideal Customer Profile)

> Multi-segment because French Broad Chocolates runs three distinct business lines (retail, wholesale, e-commerce) that touch different buyers. Treat each segment as a separate persona with its own purchase context, message, and proof set. Where this document infers from the founder transcripts vs. relies on observable customer behavior, it says so.

---

## How to read this document

- Each segment has the same eight-field structure for cross-segment comparison.
- The **lead message** field for each segment is the one-sentence pitch — the line that should open the relevant landing page or sales deck.
- The **proof set** for each segment is the small set of facts/quotes that close the sale.
- If a segment is partially inferred (i.e., not directly named by the founders), it is flagged at the top of the segment.

## Segment 1 — DTC Chocolate Buyer (E-Commerce / Subscription)

**Status:** Confirmed — referenced repeatedly in inputs (online channel grew significantly post-Helene; bonbon subscription is an existing live offer).

### Snapshot

> The repeat e-commerce buyer who orders for themselves. Often started as a tourist who visited the Lounge or Factory, now lives elsewhere, and re-orders chocolate as a small ritual.

### Demographic + psychographic

- 35–65 years old, household income $80k+, often female-led purchase decision.
- Lives outside Asheville but has visited (or has a personal connection to someone who has).
- Treats food as a category of self-care, not as a category of guilt or restriction.
- Is the kind of person who has opinions about coffee origin, will pay $14 for a glass of natural wine, and reads Christopher Kimball or the *Sift* newsletter.
- More likely to subscribe than to one-off — the relationship is part of the value.

### What they're actually buying

Not chocolate. They're buying:
- A reliable, small ceremony in their week ("Twelve new bonbons, every month").
- Permission to slow down for five minutes on a Tuesday.
- A reason to think about Asheville without leaving home.
- A taste they cannot replicate at the grocery store.

### Buying behavior

- High repeat rate after the first box.
- Subscribes more often than one-offs once trust is established.
- Will pay shipping. Hates running out.
- Reads the website. Reads the back of the box. Will tell their sister about the brand.

### Lead message

> **"A dozen hand-crafted bonbons. Every month. Made in Asheville, sourced from named producers, designed for the five minutes after dinner that belong to you."**

(Lifted in spirit from the live subscription page; aligned with Jael's voice — see voice-profile.md §6.2.)

### Objections + how to handle

| Objection | Response (per founder positioning) |
|---|---|
| "$45/month feels like a lot for chocolate." | "We're not trying to win on price… you're paying for everything that made that bar possible." (Jael) |
| "I can get good chocolate at my grocery." | "Honest answer, you can. We're offering something fundamentally different in how it was made and what it's connected to." (Jael) |
| "What if I want to skip a month?" | "Skip anytime. Pause anytime. Cancel in two clicks." (Live page proof.) |

### Proof set

- The 12-step process page.
- Named origins on every bar.
- Jael's Mother's Day blog post (the canonical voice sample for this audience).
- Customer review density on subscription products.

## Segment 2 — Gift Purchaser

**Status:** Confirmed — gifting is an explicit primary audience in the existing brand voice guide and is a top-tier section in the live shop nav (Mother's Day, Gift Sets, Bonbon Subscriptions).

### Snapshot

> The buyer who is buying *for* somebody else and needs the gift to do work the buyer can't do alone — say "I see you," "thank you," "I'm sorry," "happy birthday," "good luck."

### Sub-personas (don't merge)

| Sub-persona | Context | What they need |
|---|---|---|
| **Special-occasion gifter** | Mother's Day, birthdays, anniversaries, Mother's Day specifically (it's already a featured collection). | A curated box that arrives ready, gift-wrapped, with a card. Beautiful enough to photograph. |
| **Host / hostess gifter** | Going to a dinner, a weekend away, meeting in-laws. | A small, dignified, transportable gift. Bars or a small box. Same-day pickup if local. |
| **Corporate / B2B gifter** | HR teams, account managers, founders saying thank you to clients/employees. | Bulk, customizable, branded option. Reliable shipping at scale. Possibly co-branded packaging. |
| **Sympathy / care-package gifter** | Sending to someone going through something hard. | A box that says "I see you" without saying it. The Jael "Being a Mom" post register. |

### Demographic + psychographic

- Range 30–70.
- Buying for emotional accuracy, not for rational value. Will pay 20–30% more if the gift "feels right."
- Researches the brand before buying — the "story behind the gift" matters.
- Will repeat-purchase if the recipient reports back ("it was so good"), driving viral attribution.

### What they're actually buying

- Permission to send something thoughtful without writing the card themselves — the brand's story does the saying.
- Confidence that the gift will arrive intact, on time, and beautifully presented.
- A small Asheville signal — *"this came from somewhere intentional, not from Amazon."*

### Lead message

> **"A gift that comes with a story. From an Asheville factory you can almost smell."**

### Objections + how to handle

| Objection | Response |
|---|---|
| "Will it arrive in time?" | Ship-by dates clearly published; same-day local pickup at the Lounge or Factory. |
| "Will it survive shipping?" | Insulated packaging in summer; the brand has a 20-year hospitality track record on this. |
| "Is the box beautiful enough?" | Photography-led product pages should answer this without copy. |

### Proof set

- Visible packaging photography (the design reference page does this well).
- A clear "shipping & freshness" page.
- Real reviews from gifters quoting recipient reactions.
- The Mother's Day collection's six women-founded local food collaboration story (a co-marketing differentiator).

### Voice fit

Jael leads. Warmth, self-disclosure, "Hi friends" register, gentle product handoff at the close.

## Segment 3 — B2B Wholesale Buyer (Retail / Specialty Grocery)

**Status:** Confirmed — wholesale is one of the three named lines of business and the channel that grew most aggressively in 2025.

### Snapshot

> The buyer at a like-minded shop — specialty grocery, cheese counter, gift shop, regional food retailer, museum store, hotel gift shop — who curates inventory by story.

### Demographic + psychographic

- Owner-operator or small-team buyer at an indie specialty shop. Sometimes a corporate buyer at a regional chain (e.g., Mast General Store, Earth Fare-style banner).
- Values: prefers brands with named producers, real founders, and a shelf-talker story they can train staff on.
- Read enough food media to know the names Dandelion / Dick Taylor / Mast Brothers — *and* knows that story-quality matters more than presence on a list.
- Margin-sensitive but not price-led.

### What they're actually buying

- A brand their customers will ask follow-up questions about, with answers ready.
- A maker they can trust to ship consistently and not blow up the relationship.
- A name they can put on a shelf-talker that will not embarrass them.

### Lead message

> **"A 20-year bean-to-bar maker from Asheville with named producers, a story your staff can tell in two sentences, and chocolate that will move."**

### What to send them

- A one-page sell sheet with: story (4 sentences max), bestselling SKUs, MSRP, MOQ, lead times.
- A samples kit. Everyone in chocolate wholesale buys after they taste.
- A reference list: 3–5 anchor wholesale accounts they recognize.
- A link to Dan's sourcing transcript or a tightened version of it — for buyers who want depth.

### Objections + how to handle

| Objection | Response |
|---|---|
| "We already carry [Dandelion / Dick Taylor / Tony's]." | "We're a different lane. They're single-line bean-to-bar makers. We do all of it — bars and bonbons and brownies and pastries — and our customers buy the full story." |
| "What's our margin?" | Provide standard wholesale terms and freight clarity. Don't over-explain. |
| "How reliable is your fulfillment?" | The post-Helene rebuild made this materially better. 2025 was specifically about wholesale reliability. |
| "Why should we carry an Asheville brand outside the southeast?" | "We ship to every zip code already." (Pull live data.) "Asheville is a story buyers know." |

### Proof set

- 20 years in market.
- 50 tons/year production capacity.
- Diverse SKU base (bars + bonbons + drinking chocolate + baking) lets one buyer hit multiple shelf placements.
- Compostable packaging meets the values screens that increasingly come up in specialty buyer programs.
- B Corp status (per the journal, FBC is a B Corp — confirm currency in the wholesale deck).

### Voice fit

Dan leads on technical/sourcing questions. Jael leads on the values fit and "why we'd be good shelf-mates."

## Segment 4 — B2B Cacao + Bulk Chocolate Buyer

**Status:** Confirmed — Jael names this specifically: *"We also sell our chocolate to other makers. So there are pastry chefs and chocolatiers, and other food manufacturers who use our chocolate."*

### Snapshot

> The buyer who isn't selling French Broad Chocolates' branded product — they're using French Broad chocolate as an ingredient in their own.

### Sub-personas

- **Pastry chef** at an independent restaurant or bakery, especially in the southeast.
- **Chocolatier** running their own confections shop — often a smaller operation that doesn't make chocolate from beans themselves.
- **Coffee roaster, distillery, or cottage food manufacturer** who wants single-origin chocolate as an ingredient (mochas, mole, infused spirits, energy bars, etc.).
- **Peer chocolate maker** who shares a container or trades cacao when shorts come up. (See: Dan's mention of co-imports with Rocka, Parliament, 24 Fort Blackbirds.)

### What they're actually buying

- Chocolate in bulk format with predictable specs.
- A vendor who answers technical questions in technical terms (Dan).
- Origin transparency they can pass along to *their* customers.
- A relationship that survives a season they can't take a full container.

### Lead message

> **"Chocolate from a maker who knows what you're doing with it — because we do it too. Bulk single-origin and proprietary recipes from the same factory that produces our own confections."**

### Objections + how to handle

| Objection | Response |
|---|---|
| "Your minimums?" | Provide them. Be honest if a buyer is below MOQ; refer them to the retail line. |
| "What's the consistency batch-to-batch?" | Dan-voice answer: "We treat cacao as the agricultural product that it is. Within a given origin, harvest-to-harvest variance is real and we will tell you when we see it." |
| "Will you make me a custom recipe?" | Triage based on volume. The factory does ~500kg dark batches, ~250kg milk; that's the practical lower bound for custom work. |

### Proof set

- Dan's full sourcing transcript is the long-form sales asset.
- In-house QA program (regular lab analysis).
- 14-year direct sourcing in Nicaragua = supply reliability story.
- Co-import willingness signals "we're a partner, not just a vendor."

### Voice fit

Dan leads, almost exclusively. This is his audience.

## Segment 5 — Café / Lounge / Factory Walk-In Customer

**Status:** Confirmed — retail is the original channel and remains one of the three lines of business.

### Snapshot

> The person who is in Asheville for a weekend or for a Saturday afternoon and wants the experience, not just the chocolate.

### Sub-personas

- **Asheville visitor** (tourist, conference attendee, wedding guest). Has a few hours and is treating chocolate as one of the must-do experiences.
- **Local regular** (Asheville resident). Shows up weekly, brings out-of-town friends.
- **Tour guest** specifically — booking the 45-minute factory tour as the main event.
- **Date / occasion party** — a small group choosing the Lounge as their evening venue (chocolate + wine + dessert).

### Demographic + psychographic

- Wide demographic range — children to retirees. The Lounge in particular is multi-generational.
- Visitors skew higher household income (Asheville-tourist baseline) but the Lounge is intentionally accessible.
- Locals span the income range — the brand is *of* Asheville, not just *in* it.

### What they're actually buying

- An experience, not a transaction. The chocolate is the souvenir of the experience.
- For tour-takers: education + entertainment + the smell of the factory + a 10% discount coupon they can use that day.
- For café-sitters: a place to sit, talk, eat ice cream and a brownie, watch chocolate being made through the glass.

### Lead message

> **"Sit down. Stay an hour. We'll make you something you've never tasted, in a building you can watch chocolate being made inside."**

### Objections + how to handle

(This audience has fewer purchase objections — they are already in the building. The job is conversion-on-site to first-purchase, and conversion-from-visit to subscription/wholesale follow-up.)

### Proof set

- The space itself. Photography matters more than copy here.
- Tour reviews (TripAdvisor, Google).
- Visible production through the glass.
- 20-year track record means parents who came in 2007 are bringing their kids in 2026.

### Voice fit

Hospitality voice — warm, present, no upsell pressure. Jael's register dominates here. The Lounge is more lounge-y; the Factory is more bright-and-sensory.

## Segment 6 — Restaurant / Hotel Partner (Foodservice + Amenity)

**Status:** Inferred from broader context. The founders haven't deeply characterized this audience in the inputs, but the brand has obvious foodservice fit (named hospitality industry, drinking chocolate, ice cream base, and the existing local relationships).

### Snapshot

> The chef, F&B director, or hotel concierge who needs a local-Asheville chocolate program for their dessert menu, in-room amenity, conference welcome gift, or wholesale-to-room program.

### Sub-personas

- **Chef-owner** at an independent Asheville restaurant — sourcing chocolate for the dessert menu or pastry program.
- **F&B director** at a regional hotel, especially boutique/independent.
- **Wedding venue / event planner** — needs a beautiful, branded, repeatable favor or amenity.
- **Concierge / GM** at a hotel — needs an in-room amenity that says "you are here" in one bite.

### What they're actually buying

- A local-source story that shows up on the menu, on the bedside table, or in the welcome bag.
- A reliable supplier with the wholesale infrastructure to fulfill on a hospitality cadence.
- Something the chef can talk about in a kitchen tour or a tasting menu.

### Lead message

> **"Asheville's chocolate maker, on your dessert menu. In your welcome amenity. In the favor on the table. From a 20-year hospitality partner who lives down the road."**

### Objections + how to handle

| Objection | Response |
|---|---|
| "Custom packaging?" | Yes, at volume. The factory does custom co-branded work; needs minimum-order conversation. |
| "Pricing?" | Tiered by volume; the wholesale program covers this. |
| "How quickly can we get product?" | Lead times depend on whether it's a SKU we already make or a custom recipe. |
| "Year-round availability?" | Yes for core lineup. Seasonal collections rotate. |

### Proof set

- Local hospitality references (current Asheville restaurant + hotel partners — list to be assembled in a future sales-collateral session).
- The Lounge experience itself as a hospitality reference — visit, see how we run a room, that's how we'll show up for yours.
- Drinking chocolate program — a strong differentiator vs. generic syrup or industrial cocoa powder.

### Voice fit

Jael leads — hospitality-to-hospitality is a relational sale. Dan supports on supply reliability and product spec.

### Gap flag

This segment needs more research before producing audience-specific content. Suggested next-session task: assemble a list of current restaurant/hotel partners and a baseline price/MOQ sheet.

## Cross-segment patterns

A few patterns that show up across all six audiences and should anchor any cross-channel messaging:

### Pattern 1 — Story-led purchase

Across all six segments, French Broad Chocolates' customers are buying *with* a story, not despite it. Even the cacao-as-ingredient buyer (Segment 4, the most utilitarian) is choosing French Broad Chocolates over commodity bulk because of who and what is behind the bag.

**Implication:** Every audience-specific page or asset should foreground a name, a place, or a moment in the first 50 words.

### Pattern 2 — Asheville is an asset, not a constraint

For three segments (DTC, Gift, Walk-in) Asheville is part of the value. For two (Retail B2B, Foodservice) it is a credible-source signal. For one (B2B Cacao) it is incidental. Lean into the geography for the first three. Don't fight it for the latter two.

### Pattern 3 — Relationship-led economics

Subscription rather than one-off. Long-term wholesale rather than transactional. Co-import rather than spot-buy. French Broad Chocolates is built to do well with high-LTV/low-CAC dynamics. The marketing should match — invest in audience depth over reach.

### Pattern 4 — Pleasure as the through-line

The single emotional through-line across every segment is pleasure-as-a-vital-thing, not as transgression. From the bonbon subscriber's Tuesday-night ritual to the wholesale buyer choosing "people-pleasing" over "connoisseur-only," the pleasure framing is universal.

> *"We need chocolate no less than we need grain. Chocolate is a food just like grain is a food. We can substitute, and who's to say that one of the principal purposes of food is not pleasure?"* — Jael, *The Southern Fork*

If a piece of audience-specific copy could read as joyless, it's wrong, regardless of which segment it's targeting.
