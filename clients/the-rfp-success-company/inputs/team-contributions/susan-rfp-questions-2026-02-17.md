## Q: What are the top 5-10 mistakes companies make when responding to RFPs?

- Not shredding the RFP and analyzing minimum qualifications, requirements, terms & conditions, or red flags that might otherwise be cause for a No Bid – failure to have an approved Go/No Go tool in place
- Not submitting questions for clarification
- Not attending non-mandatory pre-proposal conferences
- Not having a bid strategy in place and knowing their own win themes
- Failure to follow the required RFP response format, e.g., character limits, word limits, font type & size, specific margins, single vs double space, etc.
- Failure to address all parts of an RFP question/requirement
- Not clearly articulating their value proposition and "What's In It For Me", e.g., not demonstrating their differentiators and how the buyer will benefit from them
- Providing boilerplate marketing material instead of a tailored response customized to the issuing agency – this results in a generic proposal that doesn't build a connection or trust
- Not scheduling an appropriate time for peer reviews or editorial reviews

## Q: What's your philosophy on go/no-go decisions? What criteria matter most?

**My philosophy:** A Go/No Go tool is most effective and valuable when decision-making is based on objectivity and used as a guide to align with a business's strategic vision and growth model. An approved and clearly defined evaluation criterion enables focus on winnable and most relevant opportunities.

## Q: What makes a SLED RFP different from federal or commercial RFPs?

As a process, the three types of RFPs differ in regulations, standardization, registration procedures, decision-making, and contract size. The differences can be broken down like this:

| Agency Type | Regulations | Standardization | Registration | Decision Process | Contract Size | Response Writing |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **SLED** | Each state maintains its own procurement statutes and codes | RFPs include general conditions specific to the issuing state | Each state and local gov't's maintain separate vendor portal with its own rules | RFPs have firm deadlines, but decision-making is often delayed and extended | Typically smaller than Federal, with shorter response timelines – T&Cs are not as unilateral as Federal | Places emphasis on value proposition and benefits – completion of forms and word limits are common |
| **Federal** | Operates under uniform Federal Acquisition Regulations (FAR) | FAR Clauses provide standardization across all issued RFPs | Requires registering with SAM.gov | RFPs have firm deadlines, but decision-making is often delayed and extended | Large and longer-term contracts with no latitude for negotiations | Typically, rigid response guidelines utilize forms and word limits |
| **Commercial** | The least regulated and often include shorter formats and fewer mandatory requirements | Usually, flexible response formats and value rapid implementations | Typically do not have registration requirements or local preferences like SLED | Include definitive deadlines, but often offer due date flexibility | Terms vary in size, but are often willing to negotiate – are open to using proposer's MSA | Least restrictive and usually values proposer's solution characteristics |

## Q: What do evaluators actually look for that most bidders miss?

In my experience, bidders frequently miss out on the following:

- **Differentiators:** demonstrating differentiation and addressing the buyer's pain points and why they are the one to hire.
- **Evidence of understanding the buyer:** bidders frequently provide boilerplate content for responses instead of using the RFP language and speaking to the buyer.
- **Addressing all parts of the requirement or question:** Instead, bidders often provide short responses that only confirm understanding and not describing how the proposer will solve for "X".
- **Evidence of experience:** not demonstrating proof the proposer has ever done the work or has successfully completed similar projects.
- **Compliance with response format:** it is common for bidders to not follow the required response format e.g. word limits, margin size, font type, etc.
- **Building a connection:** Bidders often talk a lot about themselves and fail to personalize their proposal so that it speaks to the buyer e.g. use of "client" or "customer" instead of the agency's name or specifically stating how the agency will benefit from the bidder's solution
