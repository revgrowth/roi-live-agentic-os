# PART 1 — TEMPLATE STRATEGY

## 1.1 Template Purpose

Case studies do one job: prove that Win Strategist engagements produce real outcomes for real companies. Every other page on the site asserts; case studies prove.

A visitor landing on a T4 page is almost never a cold visitor. They arrived from a service page ("Read the Full Story →"), an industry page (for vertical-specific proof), a testimonial page, organic search for the client's name, or a sales conversation where the link was sent. They want specifics:

- What was the situation before?
- What did they actually do?
- What happened after?
- Could my company get the same result?

The template answers all four, in that order, with enough specificity that a prospect can compare their situation to the case study's situation and recognize the fit. Per Core Standards Phase 4.1: "Experience signals — case study embeds with specific results." Case studies are the primary Experience signal on the site.

Three jobs this template must do:

**First, it proves.** Named clients. Quantified before/after. Real revenue numbers. Specific contract wins. A case study without quantified outcomes reads as marketing copy — fails the information gain test per Core Standards Phase 5.2.

**Second, it routes.** Every case study cross-links UP to the service page that produced the result ("Learn more about the Evaluator's Eye Audit") and UP to the industry page where the client operates ("See more transportation RFP case studies"). One case study opens doors to two commercial pages.

**Third, it compounds.** Sixteen case studies across 30+ industries create a topical authority moat. Search engines, AI answer engines, and human visitors all treat "10+ specific named results" as a different category of credibility than "we've helped many clients succeed." The template scales this advantage.

**Strategic context:** Case studies unblock the proof sections on every service page (T3) and every industry page (T5). The T3 template currently uses an RTA Fleet excerpt with a placeholder link. When T4 ships, that placeholder becomes a real page. Every T5 industry page (Healthcare, Technology, etc.) needs at least one linked case study in its proof section. Building T4 now unlocks T3 final publication and T5 design kickoff.

## 1.2 Information Gain Brief

Per Core Standards Phase 5.2, every page requires minimum 3 information gain elements with at least one client-specific. Case studies exceed this easily — the client outcome IS information gain.

**Primary keyword (RTA Fleet reference build):** RTA Fleet RFP case study, fleet management government contract case study

**Semantic variants:** proposal audit case study, RFP audit results, fleet management RFP, transportation government contract consulting

**SERP delta:**
- Zero competitors publish quantified-outcome case studies for named SLED clients in the fleet management vertical
- Zero competitors link case studies to specific methodology applications (D.A.R.E. Method, 4x4 Framework™)
- Zero competitors include evaluator-side analysis of why the engagement worked
- Most competitor "case studies" are short paragraph testimonials without before/after data

**Information gain elements on every T4 page:**

1. **Named client** — identifiable company with verifiable existence (LinkedIn, web presence, industry recognition)
2. **Quantified before/after outcome** — specific metrics with direction of change (50% → 80%, 43% → 50%+, $500K → $1M)
3. **Named engagement** — which Win Strategist service produced the result (Evaluator's Eye Audit, Embedded Content Refinement, etc.)
4. **Specific operational detail** — what the engagement actually involved, not a marketing summary
5. **Direct client quote** — attributable to a named individual at the client company with permission
6. **Methodology link** — how D.A.R.E. or the 4x4 Framework™ was applied to THIS client's situation

**Information Gain Assessment:** HIGH — 6 elements, all client-specific. Substantially exceeds Core Standards minimum.

## 1.4 Target Audience

Unlike service pages (T3) which target two distinct personas, case studies serve a single reader type: a prospect comparing their situation to the case study's situation. They've already decided they need Win Strategist-level help. They want proof it works for companies like theirs.

The prospect reading a case study is asking:

- "Is this company like mine?" (size, industry, stage)
- "Was their problem like mine?" (proposal challenges, revenue ceiling, win rate)
- "Could I apply what worked for them?" (service type, engagement scope, investment)
- "Would I get a similar outcome?" (result magnitude, timeline, confidence)

The template answers all four questions structurally. The client identity answers "is this like me." The situation answers "was their problem like mine." The approach answers "could I do this." The outcome answers "would I get this result."

## 1.5 Conversion Psychology

Case studies operate on a different conversion logic than service pages. Service pages lead the visitor TO a decision (book a call). Case studies give visitors a LENS through which to make a decision they're already considering.

**The three psychological jobs of a case study:**

1. **Validate the visitor's situation** — "Yes, companies like yours face this exact problem."
2. **Show that the solution works on problems like the visitor's** — "Here's how a real team in your situation applied it."
3. **Quantify what success looks like** — "Here's what changed, how much, and how fast."

Case studies are read in two modes:
- **Scanning mode** — visitor is skimming to decide if the case study is relevant. Hero quote + top-line metric + industry tag decide whether they read on.
- **Deep-reading mode** — visitor is comparing case studies against their situation. They read Situation / Approach / Outcome carefully.

The template serves both. The hero answers the scan question in 3 seconds. The body sections answer the deep-read questions in 4–6 minutes.

| Visitor state | Considering a specific service | Validating a decision they're forming |

## 2.5 The Situation

> **DESIGN TEAM:** First narrative section. Describes the client's state BEFORE the engagement. Must make the visitor recognize something about their own situation. Per Core Standards Phase 4.1 Experience signals — specific operational detail, not generic pain framing.

**Section Badge:** The Situation

**H2 (RTA Fleet reference):** Good, Not Great. And the Gap Was Costing Them Contracts.

**Body Copy:**

RTA Fleet submits a lot of proposals. In a recent year, they submitted 71. That volume reflects a real RFP-driven revenue engine — fleet management software has steady demand in state and local transportation agencies.

Their finalist rate hovered around 50%. In isolation, that looked acceptable. Most teams would be happy making the shortlist on half their pursuits. But in the context of how much effort each proposal required, the math got uncomfortable: they were investing writing and review hours into proposals that reached the finalist round but didn't win.

Kevin Burdick's team couldn't pinpoint the issue. Their technical content was solid. Their compliance was clean. They were qualified — demonstrably. But evaluators were still scoring them below the winner roughly half the time. Without a way to see what evaluators were seeing, they couldn't fix what they couldn't name.

**Visual Direction:**
- Single-column narrative copy, 60–70% max width for readable line length
- A single pull-quote callout in the margin: "Their finalist rate hovered around 50% — acceptable on paper, painful in practice."
- White or light background — separates visually from the dark hero
- No imagery. This section is text-forward.

## 2.6 The Approach

> **DESIGN TEAM:** Second narrative section. Describes the engagement. What the client bought, what happened during the engagement, and specifically how The RFP Success® Company's methodology was applied to THIS client's situation. Information gain anchor per Core Standards Phase 5.2 #2 (named framework application).

**Section Badge:** The Approach

**H2 (RTA Fleet reference):** Three Past Proposals. The D.A.R.E. Method. One Week.

**Body Copy:**

RTA Fleet engaged The RFP Success® Company for an Evaluator's Eye Audit — the entry-level Win Strategist engagement. The scope: review three of their recent proposals through the evaluator's lens, document what was working and what wasn't, and deliver a prioritized roadmap for their next pursuit cycle.

RFP-expert Lisa Rehurek and the team applied the D.A.R.E. Method — the tactical system inside the 4x4 Framework™ that drives a 76%+ win rate across The RFP Success® Company's client portfolio.

The four audit dimensions applied to each of the three proposals:

- **Distinction:** Did the proposal clearly show why RTA Fleet was the lowest-risk, highest-value choice — not just qualified, but the obvious winner?
- **Answer the Questions:** Did every response directly address what the RFP asked, in the order asked, at the depth evaluators needed to score?
- **Readability:** Could an evaluator reading their 30th proposal of the week find RTA Fleet's key points quickly? Did formatting aid scanning?
- **Evidence & Authority:** Did the proposal substantiate every claim with credible, specific, relevant proof?

The audit surfaced a pattern — one that RTA Fleet couldn't see from inside their own writing process. Their technical competence was clear. Their compliance was clean. But the proposals buried their win themes under layers of required response structure. Evaluators had to hunt for the differentiation that made RTA Fleet the right choice. Most evaluators don't hunt.

**Visual Direction:**
- Single-column narrative with D.A.R.E. dimensions as an inset or bordered callout
- D.A.R.E. dimensions in a 2×2 grid or vertical list with named dimension in bold, description in body
- Light charcoal tint on the D.A.R.E. callout to separate it from narrative
- No case-specific imagery; the methodology visualization is enough
