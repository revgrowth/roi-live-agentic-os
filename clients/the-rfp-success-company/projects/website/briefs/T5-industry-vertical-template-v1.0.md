# **PART 1 — TEMPLATE STRATEGY**
## **1.1 Template Purpose**
Industry vertical pages serve commercial-investigation SEO traffic. A prospect searching "healthcare RFP consultant" has more specific intent than a prospect searching "RFP consultant" — they've narrowed by vertical, which signals a more qualified position in the buying journey. T5 captures that traffic and routes it efficiently.
Three jobs:
**First, industry pages rank for commercial-investigation keywords with vertical modifiers.** "Healthcare RFP consultant," "transportation government contract help," "education RFP services." These queries have less volume than unmodified queries but higher commercial intent. A prospect typing an industry qualifier is closer to engagement than one typing a generic term.
**Second, industry pages provide the vertical-specific proof layer that service pages cannot.** A T3 service page sells the Evaluator's Eye Audit broadly. The Healthcare industry page shows what the Evaluator's Eye Audit looks like when applied to healthcare data companies specifically — with Medecision and Edlaw Pharmaceuticals as named evidence. This vertical-specific evidence is what converts an industry-qualified prospect into a discovery call.
**Third, industry pages unlock bidirectional cross-linking with case studies (T4).** Every case study links UP to its industry page; every industry page links DOWN to its case studies. This creates topical authority clusters that search engines and AI answer engines recognize. The RTA Fleet case study gains authority from being linked from the Transportation industry page; the Transportation industry page gains authority from linking to the RTA Fleet case study. Both pages rank better because both pages exist.
**Strategic context:** Industry pages are the highest-leverage SEO pages on the site. They target long-tail commercial queries with low competition, link bidirectionally with case studies (which concentrates authority), and route qualified traffic to service pages. 12 pages at launch gives the site 12 entry points for industry-qualified prospects.

## **1.5 Conversion Psychology**
Industry pages convert through pattern recognition.
A prospect reading the Transportation industry page recognizes:
*   Their own RFP volume in the description of typical pain patterns
*   Their own competitive landscape in the named case studies
*   Their own evaluator frustrations in the vertical-specific evaluator insights
Pattern recognition produces implicit trust. The page doesn't need to sell hard — it just needs to accurately describe the reader's situation in terms specific to their vertical. When a fleet management company reads "transportation agencies often score continuity and incumbent performance more heavily than greenfield innovation," they nod. That nod is the conversion trigger.
Three principles:
1. **Specificity over breadth.** A page that says "we work with healthcare companies" converts worse than one that says "we work with healthcare data companies like Medecision, pharmaceutical companies like Edlaw Pharmaceuticals, and healthcare consulting firms like Lodestar."
2. **Evaluator insight as differentiation.** Every industry page includes at least one evaluator-side insight the prospect couldn't know from their side of the table. This is the D.A.R.E./4x4 methodology applied to the vertical — how evaluators in transportation score differently from evaluators in healthcare.
3. **Industry pain pattern naming.** The page names the specific pattern that loses proposals in this industry — not generic pain like "your proposals aren't winning." Specific pain like "transportation proposals often bury incumbent value under compliance language" or "healthcare proposals over-invest in regulatory documentation at the expense of outcome evidence."

### **Tier B — Proof-Modest (4 pages)**
**Industries:** Professional & Consulting Services, Security Services, Travel Management, Disaster Recovery
**Depth:** Full T5 template structure, but proof section displays 1 case study with an accompanying statement that additional case studies in this vertical are in progress.
**Word count target:** 2,000–2,500 words
**Design treatment:** Same T5 template; proof section slightly different visual weight (1 case card featured, 2 placeholder slots with "coming soon" treatment OR single-card spotlight).
### **Tier C — Awaiting Proof (3 pages)**
**Industries:** Cybersecurity, Staffing & HR, Telecom & Networking
**Depth:** Reduced T5 template. No proof section (replaced with cross-references to adjacent verticals where applicable). Heavier emphasis on evaluator-side insight and methodology explanation to meet information gain minimum.
**Word count target:** 1,500–2,000 words
**Design treatment:** T5 template minus the proof section. Replace proof section with "Our Approach to \[Industry\] RFPs" expanded methodology section.
**Rationale for launching Tier C pages at reduced depth:** Capturing SEO real estate now and signaling capability is worth more than waiting 6–12 months for case studies. These pages will earn rankings, generate inquiries (which produce case studies), and populate proof sections over time. Thin launch beats no launch for long-tail commercial keywords.
**Tier transitions:** When a case study lands for a Tier C industry, the page migrates to Tier B (1 case) or Tier A (3 cases). This triggers a content refresh per Core Standards Phase 15 — update \`dateModified\`, add the case study to proof section, expand pain point analysis with real client context, republish.

## **2.5 The Industry Landscape**
_DESIGN TEAM: First content section. Establishes vertical-specific context. Not generic industry description — specific pattern analysis that demonstrates The RFP Success® Company understands this vertical's RFP dynamics at depth._
**Section Badge:** The Landscape
**H2 (Transportation reference):** Transportation RFPs Are High-Volume, Pattern-Heavy, and Reward Incumbent Continuity.
**Body Copy:**
Transportation agencies — state DOTs, regional transit authorities, municipal fleet departments, school district transportation offices, and non-emergency medical transportation (NEMT) providers — issue RFPs at a higher volume than most other SLED verticals. A qualified transportation or fleet management company competes in 40–80+ pursuits per year.
That volume creates two patterns evaluators don't talk about openly:
**Evaluators see the same language submission after submission.** Fleet capabilities, safety records, compliance frameworks — the content is standardized across most responses because the RFPs ask for standardized proof. By the time evaluators reach the 20th proposal in a scoring cycle, the technical content has blurred. The submissions that score highest are the ones that break the pattern while still meeting every requirement.
**Incumbent continuity carries more weight than most outside-industry consultants recognize.** Transportation contracts involve long timelines (3–7 years), capital expenditure on vehicles and infrastructure, and institutional knowledge that's expensive to rebuild. Evaluators score "risk of transition" as a significant factor — sometimes explicitly, sometimes implicitly. Incumbent proposals that reframe tenure as active advantage (continuity, proven systems, institutional knowledge that saves the agency money) routinely defeat aggressive competitors with stronger technical claims.
These two patterns — standardized content blur and undervalued incumbent continuity — are where Win Strategist engagements produce the largest outcome shifts in transportation RFPs.
**Visual Direction:**
*   Single-column narrative, 60–70% max width
*   Two bold pull-quote callouts in margins, one for each named pattern
*   Optional: a stylized stat callout — "Transportation agencies issue an average of 40–80+ RFPs per qualified vendor per year"
*   White or light gray background

**Content — Where D.A.R.E. Meets Transportation Evaluation Criteria**

*   **Distinction buried under compliance response structure.** The audit identifies where win themes need to be surfaced within mandatory response sections.

Transportation evaluator panels typically include: procurement officer (compliance-focused), operations director (execution-focused), safety officer (risk-focused), and finance representative (cost-focused).
